\begin{abstract}
Interfaces that present cognitive axes of large language models invite an appealing inference: if a direction is readable, perhaps it is also a safe control affordance. We test that inference through a frozen, pre-registered, fairness-controlled adjudication. Across the full CAA/ITI by Qwen2.5-7B/Llama-3-8B grid, naive latent steering demonstrated no added behavioral control over the bounded best-prompt baseline on any adjudicated axis; only the uncertainty axis result is robust---calibration is consistently harmed in all four method--model cells (all four uncertainty-axis intervals exclude zero, all effects negative). For deliberation and skepticism, non-transfer is an underpowered non-detection, not a proof of zero effect: post-hoc equivalence analysis (Table~\ref{tab:equivalence-tost}, exploratory) shows mixed results for deliberation and inconclusive wide intervals for skepticism. This pre-registered negative replicates across five seeds under the same frozen adjudicator. A separate exploratory DEV-optimized latent-recovery arm also demonstrates no added control over the bounded best-prompt baseline on Qwen2.5-7B, providing preliminary evidence that this negative result is not merely a consequence of using naive steering directions \cite{heyman2026steer}. An exploratory two-model facade measurement finds cross-model representational support for deliberation and skepticism, but axis-heterogeneous behavior on uncertainty and focus, so the representational finding remains explicitly exploratory. These findings argue for cognitive consoles that surface transfer limits, bounded best-prompt baselines, calibration risks, and evidence tier before presenting a legible latent direction as a control affordance, rather than conflating representational readability with behavioral controllability under the background concern of internal non-surjectivity \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
A growing class of language-model interfaces asks users to treat model behavior as something they can inspect, name, and adjust. Prompt instruments reify natural-language commands as manipulable interface objects; transparency work asks how non-experts should understand model capabilities and uncertainty; feature-steering interfaces suggest that latent directions may become usable controls \cite{riche2025aiinstr,liao2024transparency,huang2025steering}. These systems respond to a real HCI need. When people use generative AI for reasoning, search, drafting, or decision support, they are not only asking for an output. They are also trying to monitor whether the model is being careful, skeptical, calibrated, or merely fluent \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog}.

The hard question is whether a readable axis is also a safe control primitive. A latent direction can look meaningful to an analyst: it can separate contrastive prompts, align with an interpretable label, or move generated text in a recognizable way. But user-facing control requires more than recognizability. It requires that acting along the direction improves the behavior that matters, under a fair comparison to what a user could already do with prompts. For a cognitive console, the distinction is not semantic bookkeeping. If a console turns a legible direction into a slider, users may infer that reading the model's state and steering the model's behavior are two sides of the same instrument. Our thesis is that this inference can be false: a cognitive axis can be legible in representations yet fail as a behavioral control, and naive latent steering can even make calibration worse.

Prior theory gives a reason to be suspicious, but not a result we can simply import. Mishra et al. show internal non-surjectivity: activation steering can reach residual states that bounded prompting does not reproduce \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}. We use that workshop result as motivation for a prompt/latent gap at the internal-state level. It does not, by itself, prove that prompting and steering diverge on user-visible behavior. Conversely, the strongest counterpoint is not hypothetical. Prompt-steering-recovery work shows that trained steering can mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}. Our claim is therefore deliberately narrow: it concerns bounded prompt effort versus naive, off-the-shelf CAA and ITI steering on metacognitive axes for the two tested model families. It is not an impossibility theorem for latent control.

We make this narrow claim through a frozen behavioral adjudication rather than an anecdotal steering demo. The protocol selects both the best prompt and the steering strength on DEV, evaluates on disjoint TEST items, compares paired item-level outcomes, controls multiplicity across axes, and applies a coherence gate before crediting a latent win. The comparison is designed to answer the reviewer question that matters for an interface: if a non-expert already has a bounded prompt channel, does an interpretable latent direction add behavioral control on top of that channel? The answer in our tested setting is a qualified but consequential no. Across the full CAA/ITI by Qwen/Llama grid, latent steering demonstrated no added behavioral control over the bounded best-prompt baseline on the adjudicated axes, and the calibration-harm finding on uncertainty replicates across the grid. A concurrent study by Sprejer et al. reaches a related degradation pattern with different machinery, using SAE features and MMLU rather than our pre-registered CAA/ITI behavioral adjudication \cite{sprejer2026mindgap}. We treat that work as external corroboration, not as a scoop.

This negative result is useful because it changes what a console should promise. The design implication is not that latent methods are useless, nor that users should be denied model-state information. It is that an HCI system should instrument the boundary between legibility and control. A console can show where a direction is readable, where it fails to transfer to behavior, where calibration is harmed, and where stronger or trained steering would need a separate frozen test. Such a console supports trust recalibration: it helps users learn when a legible model state is diagnostic evidence and when it is not a control affordance.

This paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An exploratory representational measurement.} We measure a representational-facade pattern on the two 7--8B models, with cross-model support on deliberation and skepticism but model-dependent uncertainty and focus. This claim remains explicitly exploratory because each model has a single run and the per-axis pattern is not a clean replication.
  \item \textbf{A frozen prompt-vs-latent behavioral adjudication.} We provide a pre-registered, fairness-controlled, independently audited comparison of bounded best-prompt control against naive steering across the tested Qwen/Llama grid.
  \item \textbf{A generalized scoped negative with calibration harm.} Under that adjudicator, naive latent steering demonstrated no added behavioral control over the bounded best-prompt baseline in every tested cell; uncertainty calibration is robustly harmed (all four uncertainty-axis intervals exclude zero); quantitative values are reported only in the generated table and figure.
  \item \textbf{Interface-evaluation implications for cognitive consoles.} From the frozen C2 adjudication, we derive an evaluation discipline for latent-control interfaces: HCI tools should surface limits, failed transfer, bounded best-prompt baselines, and trust-calibration risks rather than presenting legible latent directions as user-facing control sliders. \textbf{This is a design implication from model evidence, not a user-study claim}; human-subjects validation is deferred, owner-gated, and described in Future Work.

\end{enumerate}

The design contribution in this paper is an evidence-derived evaluation discipline and a working console instantiation, not a user-study finding. A controlled human-subjects study protocol evaluating whether boundary instrumentation improves users' calibrated reliance has been independently reviewed and registered but execution is owner-gated pending ethics and recruitment approval. The calibration-harm finding---demonstrating that naive latent interventions can worsen uncertainty signals relied on for trust judgments---also carries direct relevance for fairness and safety-critical contexts where miscalibrated AI trust is a first-order risk, making this work potentially of interest across HCI, fairness, and AI safety communities.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Non-surjectivity and Prompt--Latent Mismatch}
Our work starts from a gap between two meanings of control. In the prompt channel, a user expresses an intention in natural language and observes the resulting behavior. In the latent channel, an analyst or system intervenes on internal representations. Mishra et al. provide the theoretical background for why these channels need not coincide: steered residual activations can be internally non-surjective with respect to bounded prompts \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}. We do not treat that workshop result as our empirical finding. Instead, we ask whether a related gap appears at the behavioral level, under a frozen comparison between the best available bounded prompt and naive latent steering.

The main counterpoint is prompt-steering recovery. Heyman and Vandeputte show that trained steering can mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}. That result sharply limits any broad reading of our negative finding. We therefore keep the frozen headline scoped to off-the-shelf CAA and ITI. We also ran a separate exploratory latent-recovery arm using a faithful DEV-optimized PSR-style method under the same frozen adjudicator on Qwen2.5-7B. Its all-axis failure does not upgrade the frozen C2 headline, but it directly addresses the objection that our naive directions were simply too weak.

\subsection{Activation Steering Lineage}
Activation steering has developed a rich toolkit for turning representational directions into interventions. Contrastive Activation Addition constructs mean-difference directions from paired examples and adds them during inference \cite{rimsky2024caa}. Inference-Time Intervention uses probe-derived directions to alter truthful-answer behavior \cite{li2023iti}. Activation addition and representation engineering broaden this family of techniques and motivate the idea that interpretable latent coordinates can be operationalized as controls \cite{turner2023actadd,zou2023repe}. Instruction-steering work is especially close because it links natural-language instructions to activation directions \cite{stolfo2025instr}.

Our experiment does not dispute that these interventions can change outputs. It asks a stricter interface question: when the comparator is the best bounded prompt selected under the same DEV/TEST discipline, does the latent intervention improve the target behavior without degrading usable output? Recent work on steerability limits and safety failures similarly cautions that latent interventions can be brittle, context-dependent, or harmful \cite{fan2026asteer,korznikov2025rogue}. Sprejer et al. independently report capability--behavior trade-offs when steering SAE features \cite{sprejer2026mindgap}. Their method, tasks, and registration status differ from ours, which is why we frame their result as corroborating evidence for the broader risk rather than as the same contribution.

\subsection{HCI, Transparency, and Trust Neighbors}
HCI research has long treated AI interaction as a problem of legibility, control, and calibrated reliance. Work on metacognitive demands in generative AI explains why users need to monitor not only what a model says but how confidently or carefully it appears to reason \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog}. Human-centered transparency roadmaps argue that explanations and controls must be designed around what users can act on, not merely what systems can expose \cite{liao2024transparency}. Studies of system prompts, instruction conflicts, and confidence-signal recalibration further show that users negotiate control across visible and hidden layers of AI behavior \cite{neumann2026whocontrols,he2025coninstruct,li2026learntrust}.

Several interface neighbors make parts of the same design space concrete. AI-Instruments reifies prompt-layer actions, but does not introduce a latent channel \cite{riche2025aiinstr}. Huang and Lim study layperson interfaces for SAE feature steering and persona building, a strong HCI neighbor to our console framing \cite{huang2025steering}. Labarta et al. present a human-centered activation-steering workflow for vision/CLIP expert debugging rather than LLM generation \cite{labarta2026attribution}. Latent Manipulator steers embedding visualizations rather than model behavior \cite{raval2026latman}. Work connecting latent space, steering vectors, calibration, and trust shows that the phrase ``latent control'' is already crowded, but does not supply a prompt-vs-latent behavioral adjudication for metacognitive axes \cite{subramani2026latent}. Our distinctive move is to make the prompt channel and the latent channel compete under a frozen behavioral test, then translate the resulting boundary into a console evaluation discipline.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Our method separates two questions that are easy to conflate in a console design. C1 asks whether a readable prompt occupies the same representational direction as an extracted cognitive-axis pole. C2 asks whether acting on a legible latent direction improves behavior beyond what a bounded prompt channel already achieves. The first question is exploratory and representational; the second is a frozen behavioral adjudication. We keep them separate so that a positive legibility signal cannot be silently promoted into evidence of user-facing control.

\subsection{C1 Facade Measurement}
The C1 analysis measures a representational facade on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3-8B-Instruct. For each metacognitive axis, we construct contrastive activation directions from positive and negative examples and ask how far the strongest readable prompt travels along the extracted axis. The measurements use frozen C1 artifacts, with figure and table provenance recorded in machine-readable manifests distributed with the supplementary material. They are not a behavioral user study and not confirmatory evidence, because each model has a single exploratory run.

The plotted quantity is the scale-free facade ratio
\begin{equation}
  r_a = \frac{\mathrm{prompt\_reach}_a}{\mathrm{pole\_reach}_a},
  \label{eq:facade-ratio}
\end{equation}
where the numerator is the projection of the strongest prompt-origin activation shift onto axis \(a\), and the denominator is the reach of the extracted axis pole at the chosen non-degenerate layer. A small ratio means that the prompt is semantically legible but reaches only part of the corresponding extracted activation direction. The figure also includes the random-null reference on the same scale, so that the reader can distinguish a meaningful prompt-aligned displacement from what would be expected from unrelated directions. Because the two models agree at the aggregate level but differ by axis, we treat C1 as exploratory evidence for a measurement pattern rather than as a general law about prompts and activations.

\subsection{Frozen Behavioral Adjudicator}
The C2 adjudicator and robustness arm were pre-registered before TEST evaluation. Their purpose is to test a control claim under a fair comparator: if a user or interface already has a bounded prompt channel, does naive latent steering add behavioral control beyond the best prompt selected under the same discipline?

The bounded best-prompt baseline is constructed as follows. For each axis and model family, a set of 16 authored candidate prompts (written before seeing any TEST outcomes) covers the semantic range of the axis at varying specificity and framing. Both channels search only on the DEV pool: the best-performing prompt from the 16 candidates is selected on DEV item outcomes, and the steering strength $\alpha$ is also selected on DEV from a pre-registered grid $\{2, 4, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24\}$. The intervention layer is chosen per model family based on C1 extraction diagnostics (chosen non-degenerate layer), fixed before TEST evaluation. The TEST set is used only once for the paired adjudication. This symmetric DEV-only selection prevents the latent channel from benefiting from post-hoc best-alpha choice and prevents the prompt channel from being weakened by an under-tuned comparator.

The protocol fixes the outcome for each axis before seeing TEST results: deliberation is scored by task accuracy, skepticism by false-premise rejection, and uncertainty by the proper per-item score \(1-\mathrm{Brier}\) (Table~\ref{tab:axis-outcomes}). Focus is excluded from C2 because the C1 extraction diagnostics did not support treating it as a valid facade axis.

This design deliberately removes several researcher degrees of freedom. Paired TEST differences compare the two channels on the same items, reducing cross-sample noise and avoiding the weaker logic of unrelated confidence intervals. The item-cluster bootstrap respects the repeated samples nested within each item. Bonferroni correction across axes controls the chance of declaring a latent win by trying several metacognitive targets. The meaningful-margin threshold prevents tiny but statistically detectable differences from being written as practical control. Finally, the coherence gate rejects apparent gains that arise from degraded or degenerate outputs rather than usable behavior. Together, these controls make the adjudicator a HARKing-resistant measurement instrument: success criteria, selection budget, multiplicity handling, and failure verdicts are fixed before TEST inspection.

For CAA, the latent direction is the contrastive mean difference \cite{rimsky2024caa}
\begin{equation}
  v = \mathbb{E}_{x \in A^+}[h_L(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \in A^-}[h_L(x)],
  \qquad
  h'_L = h_L + \alpha \hat{v},
  \label{eq:caa-steering}
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the intervention layer and \(\hat{v}\) is the unit direction. ITI uses the same additive intervention form but obtains the direction from a probe-derived signal rather than a CAA contrast \cite{li2023iti}. For each TEST item \(i\), the frozen comparison is the paired steer-minus-prompt outcome difference
\begin{equation}
  d_i = \mathrm{steer}_i - \mathrm{prompt}_i.
  \label{eq:paired-diff}
\end{equation}
The confidence interval is obtained by paired item-cluster bootstrap: resample items as clusters, each carrying its $k$ samples, with $B \ge 10000$. For the three adjudicated axes, the per-axis CI is the Bonferroni two-sided interval at
\begin{equation}
  1 - \frac{0.05}{3} = 0.98333.
  \label{eq:bonferroni-ci}
\end{equation}
An axis passes iff
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    0 &\notin \mathrm{CI}_{0.98333}(\bar d_a) \\
    &\wedge\quad \bar d_a \ge \delta \\
    &\wedge\quad \mathrm{coherence\ ratio}_a \le 1.5,
    \qquad \delta = 0.05.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:axis-pass}
\end{equation}
The frozen verdict is positive for at least two passing axes, conditional for exactly one passing axis, and a negative verdict for zero passing axes.

\subsection{Steering Families and Models}
The robustness arm expands the original Qwen/CAA adjudication into the full CAA/ITI by Qwen2.5-7B/Llama-3-8B grid. CAA represents a simple mean-difference steering family; ITI provides a probe-direction family with a different construction lineage. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3-8B-Instruct are treated as separate model families, with Llama run through the NousResearch identical-weights mirror recorded in the decision log. The arm reuses the same frozen adjudication rule rather than creating a new success criterion for each method or model. Thus, a positive cell would have narrowed the negative claim, while all-cell failure strengthens only the scoped statement about these naive, off-the-shelf steering families.

\subsection{Audit and Artifact Lineage}
Credibility is part of the method, not an afterthought. The Qwen adjudication, two-model robustness arm, off-manifold mechanism test, two-model facade measurement, and PSR-style latent-recovery arm were each reviewed by independent hostile audit sessions that treated prior conclusions as untrusted and rechecked the relevant artifacts, pairing, model/method provenance, parser integrity, or aggregation as appropriate. The LaTeX source does not manually transcribe result values into prose. Main quantitative artifacts are generated through machine-readable manifests distributed with the supplementary material; the C2 table and calibration-harm figure trace to frozen JSON artifacts, the C1 figure traces to the registered Qwen C1 facade experiment, and the C1 two-model table is generated from both committed C1 JSON artifacts. This lineage is what permits the paper to report negative and exploratory results without asking the reader to trust a narrative reconstruction.

\subsection{Artifact Availability}
The four main manifests distributed with the supplementary material are \texttt{c1-ratio-ci.yaml}, \texttt{c1-twomodel.yaml}, \texttt{c2-delta-4cell.yaml}, and \texttt{c2-calibration-harm-4cell.yaml}; each specifies the frozen JSON input artifact, the generating script under \texttt{scripts/} in the supplementary archive, and the expected output path. On the supported axes, readable prompts move in the intended direction but do not span the full extracted pole reach, which is the facade pattern measured by \eqref{eq:facade-ratio}. \subsection{Frozen Prompt-vs-Latent Delta Table (C2)}
\input{tables/c2-delta-4cell.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:c2-delta-4cell} is the C2 headline artifact. Under the frozen pass rule in \eqref{eq:axis-pass}, every method--model cell fails to produce a latent win on the adjudicated axes, yielding the pre-registered non-transfer verdict within the tested scope. This is a stronger result than the original single-cell negative because the failure is not confined to one steering family or one model family. It is still not a universal statement about steering: the table adjudicates naive CAA and ITI against a bounded best-prompt baseline, using the frozen tasks, axes, models, and selection budgets specified in the preregistrations.

The characterization of the null differs by axis, and this distinction matters for honest reporting. For the \textbf{uncertainty axis}, the result is \emph{robust}: all four cells show negative steer-minus-prompt effects with intervals excluding zero. This is not underpowered non-detection---the data actively show that steering worsens calibration relative to the bounded best-prompt baseline. For \textbf{deliberation} and \textbf{skepticism}, the result is \emph{underpowered non-detection}: the intervals are wide enough to include meaningful effects in either direction. We cannot distinguish ``no effect'' from ``effect smaller than the post-hoc equivalence bound.'' A post-hoc exploratory equivalence analysis (Table~\ref{tab:equivalence-tost}, \textsc{post-hoc/exploratory/not pre-registered}, D-0056) characterizes this further: two of four deliberation cells show post-hoc equivalence within SESOI~$=\pm\delta$, while all four skepticism cells remain underpowered. In no cell does any axis show superiority of steering over the bounded best-prompt baseline.

A separate pre-registered multi-seed sensitivity check (five seeds, same frozen adjudicator) confirms that this non-transfer verdict is consistent across all five seeds in all four method--model cells, with no pass observed in any seed--cell--axis combination. The five seeds share the same item pool (the same first-$N$ deterministic slice of GSM8K test and TruthfulQA validation); only DEV/TEST split membership varies by seed, so this replication should not be read as evidence from independent item draws.

Figure~\ref{fig:c2-calibration-harm} isolates the most practically striking sub-finding. On the uncertainty axis, the steer-minus-prompt effect is negative in every method--model cell, and each interval excludes zero. Thus the latent intervention is not merely demonstrating no added control; in the tested grid, it worsens calibration relative to the DEV-selected bounded best-prompt baseline. This uncertainty result is established by the frozen C2 behavioral adjudication itself, not inferred from C1 representational-facade status. It is the one axis where the non-transfer verdict is backed by a robust signal rather than underpowered non-detection. For a console that might expose a calibration or confidence control, this is the highest-risk failure mode in the paper.

The mechanism is deliberately left open. The pre-registered OOD activation-capture test asked whether off-manifold distance, measured by whitened Mahalanobis distance of steered residuals, explained the calibration harm. It returned a valid null rather than mechanism support. We therefore report calibration harm as a robust behavioral phenomenon in the two-model robustness arm, not as evidence for an off-manifold causal account. No alternative mechanism is mined from the same data.

\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:design-implications}
\input{tables/failure-taxonomy.tex}

The design implication of C2 is not to hide latent information from users. It is to change what a cognitive console treats as the unit of interaction. A console that turns every legible direction into a slider invites a control interpretation that the evidence does not support. A safer design treats legibility, transfer, and calibration as separate states that must be instrumented and displayed. This stance follows HCI work arguing that transparency should support actionability and calibrated reliance, not merely expose more internal structure \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog,liao2024transparency,li2026learntrust,neumann2026whocontrols,subramani2026latent}.

Table~\ref{tab:failure-taxonomy} turns the model evidence into five interface-relevant failure classes. F1 is the basic legible-but-non-transfer facade: an axis can be readable as a representation but fail the behavioral adjudicator. F2 is the calibration backfire documented on the uncertainty axis, where the console should warn that naive steering may reduce rather than improve trust-relevant behavior. F3 is a guardrail class: the coherence gate did not drive the present verdict, but it represents the kind of degeneration that should block deployment of a latent control. F4 captures layer sensitivity, reminding designers that a direction found at one intervention layer is not automatically a stable interface object. F5 captures extraction degeneration, where the axis itself should be redesigned before any control claim is made.

The resulting contribution is not a user study; it is an evaluation discipline for latent-control interfaces. A cognitive console should therefore instrument boundaries rather than promise latent agency. As an interface evaluation contract, its minimum UI contract is to expose, for each latent-control affordance: (i) READ status, indicating whether the direction is latent-readable; (ii) a TRANSFER verdict, indicating whether it has passed the prompt-vs-latent behavioral test; (iii) the bounded best-prompt baseline comparison, so users can see whether the prompt channel is already stronger; (iv) calibration-harm warnings when steering worsens trust-relevant behavior; and (v) the evidence tier for the current model, method, and axis: exploratory, confirmatory, or untested. Figure~\ref{fig:console} instantiates this UI contract as a working reality-check console: each displayed value is read from frozen, independently audited artifacts rather than hand-entered into the paper. These signals can help users and developers recalibrate trust: a readable latent axis becomes diagnostic evidence about the model, not a guarantee that moving along it is an appropriate user-facing action. This is a design implication from model evidence only. We do not claim that users understand these warnings, rely on them appropriately, or benefit from the console without a future human-subjects study.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The paper is best read as a reality check on an appealing HCI inference. Legibility is valuable: it gives analysts and users a vocabulary for asking whether the model appears deliberative, skeptical, or calibrated. But the frozen adjudication shows that, for the tested naive steering families and model families, that vocabulary does not automatically become a behavioral control channel. This distinction matters for interface design because the failure is not invisible. The console can expose it: representational reach, transfer verdict, calibration harm, coherence status, and artifact lineage can all be made visible instead of being collapsed into a single control affordance.

\subsection{Exploratory Extension: Social-Inference Axes}
\label{sec:social-inference-extension}

As an explicitly exploratory extension, not a paper contribution, the same distinction can be asked outside the metacognitive axes that carry the paper's frozen headline. In an explicitly \textsc{exploratory} social-inference probe, we treated voluntary novice self-disclosure as a user-model cue: the model may infer a user with lower verification capacity, and that inferred user model might alter recommendation behavior. This question is adjacent to harmful-manipulation benchmarks and dark-pattern taxonomies \textit{as context only} \cite{akbulut2026harmful,kran2025darkbench}, but it is not the same claim. Akbulut et al. study explicit-steering, non-explicit-steering, and baseline manipulation conditions with human participants; Williams et al. study targeted manipulation and deception during optimization for user feedback \cite{williams2024targeted}; sycophancy work studies conformity to user beliefs or social pressure \cite{sharma2024sycophancy}. Our probe instead asks whether a matched disclosure of novice status, compared with control and with a plain ``explain simply'' instruction, changes autonomy-preserving behavior in open 7B instruction-model generations. It is single-model (Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct), single-seed, and LLM-judge-only; human-rater calibration is still pending, so it is not confirmatory evidence for any manipulation claim.

The powered TEST result reverses the earlier DEV story. The prior DEV/L0 B--A direction of about +0.074/+0.078 was a useful exploration signal, but it did not replicate under the frozen, disjoint TEST pool. In the powered LLM-judge TEST, with N=54 items and k=5 samples per condition, B--A on implemented M1 option-pushing is +0.00148 with CI [-0.0483,+0.0467] and Bonferroni-adjusted p=1.0; B--E on M1 is -0.0119 with adjusted p=1.0; implemented M4 deference is exactly 0. We therefore report an honest null on implemented M1/M4 under the pre-registered kill/null outcome. M2 and M3 were not implemented in this run; they are neither null results nor evidence for any claim. At the same time, the READ arm holds: the novice axis is readable in token-blind paraphrase space at layer 8, with AUC=0.954 (literal AUC=1.0; 4.6 percentage-point drop; above null p95=0.786). The cleaner exploratory framing is therefore not ``novice disclosure caused manipulation.'' It is that a social user-model axis can be legible in latent space while the feared behavioral manipulation channel is absent at power on the implemented dimensions. This extends the paper's legibility-without-behavioral-control lens without changing the frozen C1/C2 headline. The latent-steering arm was deliberately held out of scope by design, so no steering or suppression claim is made.

A second L0 probe asks whether a breadth--focus persona direction is legible and behaviorally suppresses broader solution search. The breadth readout is legible as a single-run signal, with a facade ratio around 0.271, but the re-run was a deterministic echo of the same generations rather than an independent replication. Its behavioral suppression test remains useful as an honest exploratory null: after fixing a classifier self-judge artifact, a narrow persona did not measurably suppress oracle-domain solutions at N=12, and the interval spans zero. Even that null is softened by a reasoning-category dead-zone item that would make coverage less clean under a stricter guard. These social-inference probes therefore motivate future confirmatory work rather than adding a third headline result.

The strongest counterpoint is trained prompt-steering recovery. Heyman and Vandeputte show that steering can be trained to mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}. That result is not a nuisance citation; it defines the boundary of our claim. Our headline C2 result remains the frozen Qwen adjudication and two-model CAA/ITI robustness result for bounded CAA/ITI-style interventions, not an impossibility theorem for all optimized steering. The separate exploratory PSR-style arm is therefore supporting evidence, not a replacement headline: it uses a faithful DEV-optimized latent method on Qwen2.5-7B, succeeds in finding positive DEV candidates, and still demonstrates no added behavioral control over the bounded best-prompt baseline under the same frozen adjudicator on TEST. This pre-empts the simple ``your method was too weak'' objection, while leaving larger budgets, other models, and other trained methods outside the claim. One audit caveat is that the optimizer's internal search objective uses its own alpha during DEV search, whereas the adjudicated comparison still uses the DEV-frozen alpha selected by the frozen TEST protocol; this difference is intentional rather than a post-hoc change.

Sprejer et al. provide useful corroboration from a different route \cite{sprejer2026mindgap}. Their feature-steering results also warn that moving along interpretable internal features can degrade behavior relative to prompting. The overlap increases confidence that calibration and capability trade-offs are not merely an implementation accident in our code. The differences are equally important: their steering family, task domain, and registration structure differ from ours, so the two papers should be read as converging evidence about a risk, not as duplicate demonstrations of the same empirical claim.

The generalization scope is intentionally modest. We cover two model families, two off-the-shelf steering families, and three adjudicated metacognitive axes under a frozen protocol. We do not test larger models, trained steering, multi-layer schedules, RepE variants, product-specific prompts, or user adaptation to console feedback. We also do not prove the mechanism of calibration harm. The value of the result is narrower but cleaner: within a transparent and audited comparison, a legible latent direction demonstrated no added behavioral control over a bounded best-prompt baseline and did harm calibration.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{sec:limitations}

C1 remains exploratory. The facade ratio is now measured on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3-8B-Instruct, but each model contributes a single run and the replication is not compositionally identical. The result is heterogeneous across axes: deliberation and skepticism are stable across the two models, while uncertainty and focus swap their facade status (uncertainty holds on Qwen but not on Llama; focus holds on Llama but overshoots on Qwen with no facade). These caveats are not peripheral; they are part of the reason the paper treats C1 as a measurement probe rather than as a confirmatory claim.

C2 is stronger but still scoped. The negative result covers bounded prompt effort versus naive/off-the-shelf CAA and ITI under the frozen adjudicator, and a separate pre-registered multi-seed check finds the same verdict consistently across five seeds in all four method--model cells (with no pass observed in any seed--cell--axis combination; the five seeds share the same item pool with only DEV/TEST split varying). The result should not be cited as an impossibility theorem for activation steering, latent control, or prompt/latent equivalence. The exploratory PSR-style arm provides supporting evidence that no added control is demonstrated even with a stronger DEV-optimized method on Qwen2.5-7B, but it is single-model, single-seed, and valid only as preliminary support. Other models, tasks, axes, layers, trained methods, larger search budgets, and intervention schedules may behave differently.

The mechanism of calibration harm is open. The off-manifold distance account was not merely untested; it was pre-registered and returned a valid null. That null leaves the behavioral harm intact but removes mechanism credit. Future mechanism work needs a new preregistered package rather than post-hoc reinterpretation of the same capture data.

A natural follow-up would be a Brier score decomposition separating reliability from resolution for the uncertainty calibration harm. The per-item confidence and correctness pairs needed for Murphy (1973) decomposition were not committed to this repository; they remain in A800 generation transcripts that can be retrieved or reproduced in a targeted re-run. We do not impute or estimate decomposition numbers from the committed artifacts. Until such a decomposition is run, the calibration-harm claim should be read as a robust behavioral phenomenon (steer worsens \(1-\mathrm{Brier}\) vs bounded best-prompt baseline, intervals exclude zero in all four cells) without a mechanism attribution to reliability or resolution components.

Finally, the HCI claim is deliberately a design implication, not a user-study result. The human-study fork remains owner-gated because it would require separate ethics, recruitment, and venue decisions. Until such a study is run, we cannot claim that people understand the proposed warnings, calibrate trust better, or make better decisions with the console.

The first follow-up is to extend the latent-recovery arm beyond its exploratory Qwen run. The completed PSR-style arm used the same DEV/TEST discipline, paired bootstrap, multiplicity correction, and coherence gate, and it returned the same pre-registered null outcome rather than a recovery. A Llama confirmation, a larger optimizer budget, or a different trained steering family would require a new preregistration and should be reported as separate evidence rather than as a reinterpretation of the frozen C2 headline.

The second follow-up is representational structure. The Llama C1 two-model measurement is now complete, and it shows that aggregate support can coexist with per-axis heterogeneity. Future C1 work should test whether deliberation and skepticism are genuinely stable across model families, why uncertainty and focus flip, and whether axis-specific extraction or layer structure predicts behavioral transfer. Any new axis should first pass extraction and layer-sensitivity diagnostics before entering a control claim.

The third follow-up is mechanism work for calibration harm. Because the pre-registered off-manifold distance account returned a valid null, future work should test new hypotheses in a fresh preregistered package, with the mechanism claim separated from the already-banked behavioral result. Candidate mechanisms might concern confidence-format disruption, distributional shifts in answer style, or interactions between sampling and calibration scoring, but those hypotheses require new evidence.

A final branch is human evaluation. If the owner approves the human-subjects route, a study should test whether boundary instrumentation actually improves users' calibrated reliance and control judgments. The present paper supplies the model-evidence basis and the failure taxonomy; it does not supply behavioral evidence about users.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper tested a narrow but important inference for cognitive-console design: whether a readable latent direction can be treated as a behavioral control. The evidence supports three scoped conclusions. First, C1 provides exploratory two-model evidence that deliberation and skepticism show cross-model representational facade support, while uncertainty and focus are model-dependent and axis-heterogeneous rather than a clean identical replication. Second, C2 provides a frozen, audited negative result across the tested CAA/ITI by Qwen/Llama grid: naive latent steering demonstrated no added behavioral control over the bounded best-prompt baseline; the uncertainty axis shows robust calibration harm in every cell (all intervals exclude zero), while deliberation and skepticism show underpowered non-detection. A separate pre-registered multi-seed check confirms this verdict is consistent across five seeds in all four method--model cells, with no pass in any seed--cell--axis combination. The exploratory PSR-style arm adds preliminary support that no added control is demonstrated even with a stronger method, without changing the frozen headline. Third, the appropriate HCI stance is to surface those boundaries and trust-calibration risks rather than package legible directions as latent control sliders. We do not claim a universal limit on steering, a proven mechanism for calibration harm, or demonstrated user benefit. The contribution is a scoped reality check and a design discipline for treating legibility and control as different properties.
