\begin{abstract}
Interfaces increasingly expose a model's internal signals as controls: a probe for a named latent property, such as the model's confidence or caution, is wired to a slider the user can push. Whether such a control has earned an active role, rather than a read-only one, is a per-control release decision the field has no shared way to make. We contribute an evaluation method that judges each control against the realistic alternative available to its user population: a strong, DEV-selected prompt for a proficient user, an ordinary prompt for a non-expert. The method separates a warranted quality gain from mere convenience, and binds every verdict to an uncertainty rule, a coherence gate, and an evidence tier. Applying it to CAA and ITI steering on Qwen and Llama across three behavioral axes in a substitution design, we find that naive latent steering beats the strong prompt on none of the twelve tests, and a stronger DEV-optimized method (Qwen, exploratory) fails likewise; because a predeclared positive control still passes the same gate, this all-fail grid is a result, not a dead assay. The comparator-negative uncertainty axis is floor-independent, with its Qwen--CAA cell fully powered, and the single floor-sensitive cell's marginal positive is an artifact rather than a control. Whether such a control nonetheless helps a non-expert who would not write the strong prompt---through convenience rather than quality---is an open question for future work. The finding is specific to these techniques, yet it makes a readability--controllability gap measurable at the level of a single release decision.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{introduction}

Alex, an everyday user, is drafting a policy recommendation with an AI assistant and sees a slider labeled ``uncertainty awareness.'' Alex likes it: nudging a slider is far easier than composing the right instruction, and the assistant's tone shifts as it moves. To Alex the control is convenient, and convenience reads as useful. Priya, a colleague fluent with these tools, asks a different question: does moving the slider actually make the assistant better at separating what it knows from what it does not, beyond what a plain instruction would already achieve? A readable direction or a changed completion does not answer Priya's question, and Alex's convenience does not either.

These are two standards, not one. For an everyday user who cannot easily write a strong prompt, a low-effort, accessible slider is attractive. For a proficient user---someone who can write a strong prompt for the task, so their realistic alternative is a well-crafted instruction---a control is worth adding only if it earns incremental behavioral advantage over that prompt. So whether a slider is good depends on who is asking and against which alternative, a question output-change demonstrations and legibility both leave open.

Three precedents ground the pattern. Bo et al.~expose activation steering through SELECT, CALIBRATE, and LEARN controls; Anthropic's Golden Gate Claude made an internally modified Claude 3 Sonnet publicly available for 24 hours; and Karny et al.~show that a neural-transparency interface can raise reported trust and perceived helpfulness without changing several behavioral outcomes, a reminder that felt usefulness and measured benefit can diverge. \cite{bo2026steerable,anthropic2024goldengate,karny2026neural} {}% SOURCE_CHECKED 2026-08-11
These precedents share one move: as interpretability matures into an interface, a readable internal signal is wired to a control the user can act on. The move is already real---one public demonstration and two user-studied prototypes---if not yet a settled product pattern, which is exactly when the field needs a shared, still-absent way to decide whether a signal has earned that active role, for whom, and against what the user could already do without it. Nor is the concern fabricated: our formative expert reports that practitioners do infer a working control from a legible direction (Section~\ref{formative-study}). That release decision, not any single steering method, is this paper's subject.

We therefore ask not whether a particular latent slider is good, but \textbf{how to decide whether a readable internal signal has earned the right to act as an interface control}. Our answer is an evaluation method with a design artifact at its center, not a verdict on one technique. It rests on three commitments. First, judge a control against each user population's \emph{realistic alternative}---a strong best-of-set prompt for a proficient user, an average prompt for a non-expert---not against ``no control.'' Second, separate \emph{convenience} from \emph{warranted quality gain}, since a control can win on one and lose the other. Third, bind every result to an evidence tier so it cannot silently transfer across model, method, axis, or task. A five-field record makes these commitments operational, carrying READ (local representational support), TRANSFER (behavioral advantage over the declared alternative), the bounded prompt comparator, a calibration warning, and the evidence tier from a computational result to an explicit interface action. Unlike method-level benchmarks that rank prompting against steering \cite{wu2025axbench} or study the representation--actionability gap in aggregate \cite{basu2026actionability}, and unlike steering interfaces evaluated for preference \cite{bo2026steerable} or passive transparency \cite{karny2026neural}, our unit of analysis is one control's release decision, judged separately against each population's alternative. {}% C-001

This paper makes three contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{An evaluation method for internal-signal controls.} We give a way to decide whether a readable internal signal has earned an active interface role: judge it against each user population's realistic alternative (a strong prompt for a proficient user, an average prompt for a non-expert), separate convenience from warranted quality gain, apply a declared outcome, uncertainty rule, point-estimate floor, and coherence gate, and bind the result to an evidence tier. We instantiate it for latent steering and advance its broader applicability as a design argument.
\item
  \textbf{A five-field record that connects evaluation to interface action.} In the spirit of model cards and datasheets, but at the granularity of a single control affordance, READ, TRANSFER, the bounded prompt comparator, a calibration warning, and an evidence tier map a computational result to an explicit blocking reason and a record-specific decision, so a control is promoted, withheld, or kept read-only for stated reasons rather than on legibility or output movement alone.
\item
  \textbf{A fully worked, independently audited case study.} Applying the method to CAA and ITI on Qwen and Llama across three behavioral axes, no latent control earns an advantage over a strong prompt on any of the twelve tests, while a predeclared positive control shows the method can also qualify a control, not only withhold one---an honest, bounded negative for these techniques rather than a verdict on latent control in general.
\end{enumerate}

The case study centers on a substitution design: the latent intervention is offered \emph{in place of} a prompt. We additionally report composition tests (steering added on top of the prompt) for CAA and ITI on Qwen, which show no incremental gain---ITI if anything trends slightly negative---and composition beyond these two methods and this model is out of scope here.

\section{Formative Expert Consultation}\label{formative-study}

To ground this release-decision problem in practice and to elicit requirements for the qualification record, we conducted a formative consultation with one senior expert in human-centered computing. The participant is an associate professor with over ten years' experience in human-centered computing, service computing, and the design and evaluation of user-facing systems; they are described without identifying details, and the consultation followed the applicable ethics determination with informed consent. The session was a 50-minute semi-structured online interview (one interviewer, one notetaker) in which the expert reacted to the problem framing (a legible internal signal wired to a slider) and to a concrete stimulus, the worked Qwen--CAA uncertainty five-field record (Table~\ref{tab:worked-record}); we analyzed the notes by lightweight thematic coding and requirement extraction. At this formative stage we prioritized depth over breadth: one senior expert engaged with a detailed semi-structured protocol and a concrete worked stimulus supports the fine-grained, traceable requirements elicitation this consultation is for. It is a single-expert, non-representative, motivational exercise: it grounds the problem and shapes requirements, makes no representativeness claim, and does not test comprehension, reliance, behavior, or the interface, nor substitute for a summative user study.

The expert confirmed the pain point---that a legible, interpretable direction is readily mistaken for a working control---while qualifying it as population- and scenario-dependent: it is most acute for new interpretability-derived features, where a semantically clear internal direction invites the inference ``explainable, therefore controllable,'' and least acute where mature alternatives such as prompting or workflow design already meet the goal. Four requirements emerged. First, \emph{behavioral, reproducible gain is the release-critical evidence}: legibility is a precondition (diagnostic, not product evidence), and a control should earn an active role only on stable behavioral improvement, not on the existence of an interpretable signal, prioritizing the TRANSFER field over READ~\cite{bucinca2020proxy}. Second, \emph{the comparator must match the target population}: a strong, well-crafted prompt is the fair baseline for a proficient user and an ordinary prompt for a non-expert, with no single universal baseline, converging with the two-comparator design. Third, \emph{negative, uncertain, or inconclusive results must be shown honestly}, with scope and confidence stated, because users tend to read a control's mere presence as a validated, reliable capability and over-rely on it~\cite{lee2004trust,schemmer2023appropriate,vasconcelos2023explanations,passi2022overreliance}, supporting the calibration warning and the choice to expose the blocking reason rather than a pass/fail badge. Fourth, \emph{who benefits, and by how much, should be recorded}: the expert proposed an explicit user-value dimension capturing per-population benefit and heterogeneity, which the present five-field record captures only indirectly through its two comparators and which we flag as a refinement. Asked for one rule of thumb for withholding a slider, the expert offered: release only if the gain is stable and clearly exceeds existing methods such as a well-designed prompt, and otherwise defer, which mirrors the release rule this paper instantiates. These formative inputs motivate the method and shape the record; they are not evidence for the computational results reported later.

\section{Related Work}\label{related-work}

\subsection{From Legible Representations to Behavioral Actionability}\label{from-legible-representations-to-behavioral-actionability}

Representation-engineering methods identify and intervene on directions inside language models. Representation Engineering centers population-level representations as objects for monitoring and manipulation; Activation Addition derives lightweight vectors from contrasting prompt pairs. \cite{zou2023repe,turner2023actadd} CAA estimates directions from paired positive and negative examples, while ITI derives probe directions and modifies activations during generation. \cite{rimsky2024caa,li2023iti} Instruction-specific vectors further show positive cases in which steering improves constraint following with and without explicit instructions. \cite{stolfo2025instr} {}% SOURCE_CHECKED 2026-08-11
Together, these methods establish that internal representations can be measured and manipulated under specified conventions, and recent work explicitly frames them as levers for control and as calibrators for trust. \cite{subramani2026latent} Our focus is the next interface decision: what behavioral evidence turns such a representation into a warranted control?

Recent benchmarks make this distinction empirically consequential. AxBench evaluates prompting, finetuning, and several representation-level methods on common concepts; its official Table 2 reports a mean overall steering score of 0.894 for Prompt and 0.239 for DiffMean after factor selection, distinct from the mutable repository leaderboard. \cite{wu2025axbench} {}% SRC-007
Basu et al.~examine the gap between internal representation and actionable output correction in 400 clinical vignettes, including a safety-focused prompt-engineering condition in Arm 1; for Qwen, sensitivity changes from 0.451 under the original parser to 0.729 under the refined parser, showing that the measured gap is parser-sensitive. \cite{basu2026actionability} {}% SRC-008
These studies establish the empirical premises for our contribution; we operate at the next unit of analysis: one proposed interface control, its declared comparator, and the evidence attached to its release decision.

Internal reach and behavioral advantage are distinct design quantities. Mishra et al.~show that steering can reach residual states outside a bounded prompt image. \cite{mishra2026nonsurj} Heyman and Vandeputte show that trained steering can mimic prompting, while Sprejer et al.~reveal capability--behavior trade-offs for feature steering relative to prompting. \cite{heyman2026steer,sprejer2026mindgap} Together, these results motivate a local behavioral qualification record rather than an inference from internal geometry alone.

Work on metacognitive demands and human-centered transparency frames exposed information relative to a task and decision. \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog,liao2024transparency} {}% SOURCE_CHECKED 2026-08-11
We operationalize that principle as READ, TRANSFER, and interface eligibility: local representational evidence, comparator-bound behavioral evidence, and the resulting interface action.

\subsection{Prompting as a Declared Behavioral Alternative}\label{prompting-as-a-declared-behavioral-alternative}

Prompting is both a practical interaction channel and a technical comparator. Prompt-derived AI instruments can reify intent as reusable direct-manipulation controls, while studies of non-expert prompt design show that constructing and evaluating prompts is itself difficult. \cite{riche2025aiinstr,zamfirescuPereira2023johnny} AxBench directly places prompting beside representation-level methods. {}% SRC-007
Bo et al.~compare steering interfaces with an unscaffolded prompt baseline and separately test prompting on top of steering before their user study. \cite{bo2026steerable} {}% SRC-009
Basu et al.~include prompt engineering within a set of mechanistic intervention arms. {}% SRC-008
These works make prompting an established comparison channel. Our contribution is to bind that channel to a per-affordance interface decision, and to split it by population rather than rank steering methods globally.

A single prompt comparator hides the population question; we therefore use two, a strong best-of-set prompt for the proficient user and an average prompt for the non-expert (defined in Method). {}% FL-008
{}% FL-009
These are proxies for two user populations rather than an invented pair: non-experts systematically find prompts hard to construct and evaluate. \cite{zamfirescuPereira2023johnny} Whether the candidate-set average matches what a novice actually writes is an assumption future work would need to check. Reporting a latent intervention against both makes ``better than a prompt'' precise about which prompt, and thus about whom the control would help.

The current application uses substitution: the latent intervention replaces the prompt channel. {}% FL-015

\subsection{Mechanistic-Interpretability Interfaces and Human Evidence}\label{mechanistic-interpretability-interfaces-and-human-evidence}

HCI work has begun turning internal model representations into visible or manipulable interface elements. Bo et al.~instantiate three steering designs---SELECT, CALIBRATE, and LEARN---and report heterogeneous preferences in an exploratory within-subject study. \cite{bo2026steerable} {}% SRC-009
Other systems expose feature steering, attribution-guided intervention, or embedding manipulation to people. \cite{huang2025steering,labarta2026attribution,raval2026latman} {}% SOURCE_CHECKED 2026-08-11
This literature establishes latent representations as interaction objects; we add the qualification step that connects computational evidence about one such object to its blocking reason and interface action.

Public demonstrations extend this trajectory beyond research prototypes. Anthropic altered the strength of an internal Golden Gate Bridge feature in Claude 3 Sonnet and made the modified model available through \texttt{claude.ai} for 24 hours. \cite{anthropic2024goldengate} {}% SOURCE_CHECKED 2026-08-11
Our target is the evidence policy that follows these technical and interface precedents: what must one named control establish before it passes a computational eligibility gate?

Karny et al.~provide the closest verified interface and human-evidence neighbor. Their neural-transparency system projects a system prompt onto persona trait vectors and presents the predictions through an interactive sunburst before users chat with their configured model. In a controlled between-subjects study, the visualization increased endpoint trust and was rated helpful, but it did not produce significant differences in prompt iteration, resulting persona scores, message engagement, or confidence in predicting unintended behavior. \cite{karny2026neural} {}% SRC-010
They study passive prediction and explicitly leave active steering interfaces to future work. Their subjective/behavioral divergence also cautions against treating perceived usefulness as evidence that an interface improves actionability judgments.

Documentation frameworks such as model cards and datasheets show how a structured record can bind claims to provenance, evaluation, and scope, and algorithmic-audit frameworks add a process-oriented precedent in which staged development decisions produce linked records across the system lifecycle. \cite{mitchell2019modelcards,gebru2021datasheets,raji2020accountability} {}% SOURCE_CHECKED 2026-08-11
These records account for a model, a dataset, or a development lifecycle. Our five-field record carries the same accountability move down to the finest interface unit---a single control affordance---and adds what documentation alone omits: a behavioral qualification rule that turns the record from a description into a release decision, capturing READ, TRANSFER, the bounded comparator, a calibration warning, and an evidence tier for one proposed active latent control. \subsection{Positioning Summary}\label{positioning-summary}

Prior work supplies the ingredients but not their combination at this unit of analysis. We claim neither the first prompt--steering comparison nor the first interpretability--actionability gap; our contribution is to combine these into an evaluation method for a single control's release decision, binding a per-population realistic-alternative comparator, an explicit split between convenience and warranted quality gain, an uncertainty and coherence rule, and an evidence tier to one model, method, axis, and task, and carrying the verdict into a record-specific interface action. {}% OI-009

\subsection{Five-Field Qualification Record}\label{five-field-qualification-record}

The record contains five fields.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{READ} specifies the model, method, direction construction, layer, task, and measurement under which an internal axis is legible. READ is a diagnostic precondition, not behavioral evidence.
\item
  \textbf{TRANSFER} records the behavioral outcome, intervention, estimand, uncertainty, point-estimate floor, and coherence result.
\item
  \textbf{BOUNDED PROMPT COMPARATOR} identifies the declared behavioral alternative, candidate set, and selection procedure. In this application it is a DEV-selected prompt drawn from a preregistered bounded set of sixteen candidates; the field preserves the provenance limits detailed below. {}% BD-009
\item
  \textbf{CALIBRATION WARNING} states which contrast is being interpreted---for example, steer versus prompt rather than steer versus an unsteered baseline---and records missingness or scoring threats that can reverse the reading.
\item
  \textbf{EVIDENCE TIER} binds the record to a model, method, axis, task, layer, protocol, and evidence status so that a result cannot silently transfer to another setting.
\end{enumerate}

The record uses a direct hybrid mapping: computational results first, then a structured evidence or blocking reason, then interface eligibility and action (Figure~\ref{fig:evidence-flow}). READ may be supported, unsupported, or not evaluated. TRANSFER may be not tested, not evaluable, no-pass, or pass. The record preserves why: the interval includes zero, the contrast is comparator-negative, the estimate is below the point-estimate floor, coherence fails, or missingness limits interpretation. READ support makes the representation a \textbf{read-only diagnostic candidate within this evidence tier}. An active control passes the computational gate within this evidence tier only when READ is supported and the reported TRANSFER rule passes. In every other case the active control is withheld, without turning the blocking reason into a formal state or an operational claim. {}% FL-077
{}% OI-014

\subsection{Models, Methods, and Behavioral Axes}\label{models-methods-and-behavioral-axes}

The worked application uses Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct, with the latter loaded from the NousResearch identical-weights mirror after gated access blocked the canonical repository. {}% FL-011
{}% MODEL_IDS: Qwen/Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct; NousResearch/Meta-Llama-3-8B-Instruct
Both run in CUDA float16. The committed artifacts record the selected hidden-state layers by deliberation/skepticism/uncertainty: Qwen--CAA uses 20/20/20, Qwen--ITI uses 20/19/17, Llama--CAA uses 12/14/10, and Llama--ITI uses 8/14/11. The intervention hooks the output of decoder block \(L-1\), which corresponds to Hugging Face hidden state \(L\), and adds the direction at every token position during prefill and generation. Activation extraction renders one user turn through the model chat template and pools the last non-padding token. Immutable upstream model revision hashes were not recorded.

CAA uses a positive-minus-negative mean-difference direction that is unit-normalized before injection. {}% FL-023
{}% FL-024
ITI fits a logistic probe to the same 28-pair extraction split, selects a nondegenerate layer no shallower than 20\% of model depth, unit-normalizes the probe direction, and multiplies it by the frozen projected-activation sample standard deviation. {}% FL-025
Both methods select a coefficient from \(\{2,4,6,8,12,16,24\}\) on DEV, but equal coefficients do not imply equal injected norms: ITI injects \(\alpha\sigma_L\), so no common norm bound of 24 applies. {}% FL-012
{}% FL-026

The reported CAA convention is

\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
v_a &= \mathbb{E}_{x\in A_a^+}[h_L(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\in A_a^-}[h_L(x)],\\
\hat v_a &= v_a/\lVert v_a\rVert, \qquad h'_L=h_L+\alpha\hat v_a.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:caa-steering}
\end{equation}

The common additive notation preserves method-specific scale:

\begin{equation}
h'_L=h_L+\alpha s_{m,a,L}\hat u_{m,a,L},\qquad
s_{\mathrm{CAA},a,L}=1,\quad s_{\mathrm{ITI},a,L}=\sigma_{a,L}.
\label{eq:method-scaling}
\end{equation}

% FL-023–FL-026

The three TRANSFER axes use task accuracy for deliberation, false-premise rejection for skepticism, and \(1-\mathrm{Brier}\) for uncertainty awareness. {}% FL-018
These operational outcomes do not exhaust the psychological constructs named by the axes. For uncertainty, both conditions request an answer and confidence. The deterministic parser accepts \texttt{Confidence:}/\texttt{conf} forms, interprets values above one as percentages, clamps them to \([0,1]\), and imputes missing confidence as 0.5, which makes \(1-\mathrm{Brier}=0.75\) regardless of correctness. {}% FL-019
{}% FL-022
The scorer and parser are implemented in \texttt{src/cognitive\_console/eval/scorers.py} and exercised by the repository test suite.

\subsection{Two Prompt Comparators and Substitution Design}\label{two-prompt-comparators-and-substitution-design}

The preregistered bounded candidate set contains sixteen instructions with varied wording and specificity. From it we derive two comparators, one per population. The \textbf{best-of-set prompt} is selected on DEV for each model and axis and stands for the proficient user's realistic alternative; this is the comparator behind all frozen results reported here. {}% FL-008
{}% FL-009
The \textbf{average prompt} is the per-item mean outcome over the candidate set, used as a proxy for what a non-expert is likely to write (an operationalization future work would need to check); it is evaluated by a preregistered comparator arm on Qwen (reusing the frozen instrument, direction, and coefficient), reported in Section~\ref{everyday-user-comparator}. A separately generated novice-style prompt comparator is reported as exploratory context in Appendix~\ref{app-novice}.

\subsection{Reported Qualification Rule}\label{reported-qualification-rule}

For axis \(a\), let \(\bar d_a\) be the paired mean difference between steering and the selected prompt, \(I_a\) its item-cluster bootstrap interval, \(g_a^S\) the steering degradation score, and \(g_a^0\) its baseline counterpart. The reported rule qualifies an axis only when

\begin{equation}
\operatorname{qualify}(a)\iff
0 \notin I_a\ \land\ \bar d_a\ge\delta\ \land
g_a^S\le\tau g_a^0+\epsilon.
\label{eq:qualification-rule}
\end{equation}

with \(\delta=0.05\), \(\tau=1.5\), and \(\epsilon=0.02\). {}% FL-030
{}% FL-031
{}% PREREG: docs/ledgers/prereg-c2b-adjudication.md; IMPLEMENTATION: src/cognitive_console/experiments/adjudicate_c2b.py
Equation \ref{eq:qualification-rule} requires an interval excluding zero and a point estimate at least \(\delta\); it does \textbf{not} require the interval's lower bound to exceed \(\delta\). This is a positive-advantage release criterion: an interval excluding zero with a point estimate that clears \(\delta\), without demanding the largest defensible effect. A stricter release can raise \(\delta\) or require the lower bound to clear it. We set a release bar rather than a scientific one: in this application \(\delta=0.05\) is the minimal behavioral advantage we require before adding a control, and the coherence gate screens for repetition degradation. Under an explicit conservative assumption that a wrongly released control is costlier than a withheld one, the rule favors withholding when the evidence is uncertain. The portable requirement is a declared comparator, outcome, uncertainty rule, floor, and coherence gate; the specific values (\(\delta=0.05\), the coherence ratio, and the two-axis aggregation) are this application's settings, not part of the portable procedure.

The protocol and executable adjudicator were frozen on 2026-07-23. The degradation score is one minus the distinct trigram ratio for each generation; scores are averaged within the steering and unsteered cells, and the steering mean must satisfy \(g_a^S\le1.5g_a^0+0.02\). The implementation adds \(10^{-12}\) only as floating-point comparison tolerance.

The computational procedure labels a model--method cell positive when at least two axes pass, conditional when one passes, and negative when none pass. {}% FL-033
This cell-level result remains distinct from the structured reason and record-specific interface action.

\subsection{The Everyday-User Comparator: No Advantage Over an Ordinary Prompt Either}\label{everyday-user-comparator}

The tests so far compare steering with a strong prompt, the alternative a proficient user could write. To reach the everyday user, who is unlikely to write that prompt, we repeat the comparison against an average prompt: the per-item mean outcome over the sixteen frozen candidates, reusing the same TEST items, frozen coefficient, and adjudicator on Qwen (Llama was not run; this preregistered arm is exploratory and single-run on a single model, and is not part of the confirmatory headline).

Two facts emerge. First, the ordinary prompt is much weaker than the strong one: on skepticism the average trails the best prompt by about \(0.21\), while on deliberation the like-for-like gap is negligible near the accuracy ceiling and the uncertainty comparison is measurement-limited. Second, steering still does not clear the ordinary prompt. The steer-minus-average estimate is positive but its interval includes zero in every cleanly measured cell---CAA skepticism \(+0.126\ [-0.009,+0.261]\), ITI skepticism \(+0.106\ [-0.051,+0.266]\), and CAA deliberation \(+0.027\ [-0.054,+0.106]\)---while the uncertainty cells remain missingness-limited and the ITI deliberation cell fails the coherence gate and is not interpreted. The ordering is \emph{strong prompt} \(>\) \emph{steering} \(\gtrsim\) \emph{ordinary prompt}, with the steering-over-ordinary margin not distinguishable from zero. On quality, then, steering neither replaces the proficient user's strong prompt nor reliably beats the everyday user's ordinary one. Whether its convenience nonetheless makes it preferable for the everyday user is an open question these results do not settle and future work would need to address. A separately generated novice-style prompt comparator (Appendix~\ref{app-novice}) yields the same qualitative picture and is reported there as exploratory context only.

\section{Interface-Facing Qualification Record}\label{interface-facing-qualification-record}

\subsection{From Evaluation Output to Interface Eligibility}\label{from-evaluation-output-to-interface-eligibility}

The record is designed to guard against three substitutions in interface reasoning: READ for TRANSFER, absolute output change for comparison against the declared alternative, and evidence from one tier for another. It reports the computational result, preserves the structured evidence or blocking reason, and specifies the record-specific interface action.

\paragraph{What a designer gains, for each user.} The record's user is the interface designer or team deciding whether to release a control, and it separates the two populations that decision serves. On the proficient user's standard, it already answers: no tested control earns incremental behavioral advantage over a strong prompt, so none has yet earned an active role as a quality control for users who can write that prompt. \subsection{Future Work: Evaluating with Everyday and Proficient Users}\label{planned-evaluation}

The model evidence speaks to the proficient user's standard but not to the everyday user's, which turns on convenience rather than incremental quality. Closing that gap is future work rather than a committed study: a design with two participant groups, split by whether a participant can write a strong prompt for the task, would have them complete matched tasks with the uncertainty affordance offered as a slider, as their own prompt, and as a read-only diagnostic. On the convenience side it would measure four named facets---\emph{effort} (completion time, operation steps, and edit iterations, with a short workload rating), \emph{accessibility} (task success without crafting a prompt), \emph{discoverability} (whether the control's function and use are understood), and \emph{willingness to use} (the choice between the slider and writing a prompt, with reasons)---alongside task outcome and reliance, with two-sided hypotheses and no assumed direction. This paper reports no user data and makes no such measurement; we sketch the design to make the open question concrete, not to claim it as committed or completed work.

\section{Discussion}\label{discussion}

\subsection{Legibility Is Not a License to Act}\label{an-honest-negative-not-a-blind-assay}

The design lesson survives the negative result: a legible internal direction can be real and still behaviorally inert, so legibility alone must not license an active control. The worry that such a negative merely reflects a dead assay does not hold here. Two positive controls---a predeclared decision-rule control and an independently reproduced latent steering method (Stolfo instruction-steering on Phi-3)---pass the same frozen adjudicator and coherence gate (detailed in Section~\ref{assay-validity-and-statistical-resolution}), so the gate can say yes when the evidence warrants it. That bound is deliberately narrow: those controls certify the adjudication procedure rather than sensitivity at the three console axes on Qwen and Llama, and they neither make those axes controllable nor revise the frozen grid. Across these interventions (single-layer additive CAA and ITI on two 7--8B models), a readable signal earned no right to act, and an honest console says so rather than promoting the control on its legibility.

\subsection{READ Supports Inspection Without Presuming Actionability}\label{read-supports-inspection-without-presuming-actionability}

A readable direction can still support inspection, debugging, hypothesis formation, or a read-only trace---the worked READ results make that value concrete (Figure~\ref{fig:c1-ratio-ci}). {}% FL-039
{}% FL-040
The alternative is not ``ship a slider'' or ``hide the representation,'' but to preserve local evidence without turning visibility into a behavioral promise.

The three evidence profiles lead to different evaluation work. Where refined, skepticism is bounded-equivalent; deliberation, re-measured at length across all four cells, is a no-pass---three cells slightly negative and underpowered, the ITI--Llama cell near-zero and bounded-equivalent; uncertainty calls for measurement repair and a comparator-specific warning. {}% FL-041
{}% FL-043
{}% FL-047
The record's practical contribution is the explicit path from computational result to structured reason to record-specific interface action.

\subsection{Why the Comparator Changes the Interface Claim}\label{why-the-comparator-changes-the-interface-claim}

Choosing a comparator is choosing a population: a strong prompt asks the proficient user's question, an average prompt asks the everyday user's, and a control can clear one bar while missing the other. In the Qwen--CAA format-and-missingness recheck, the comparator field records prompt improvement under the frozen scorer over a steer-near-baseline condition rather than direct steering harm. {}% FL-044
{}% FL-045

The procedure asks designers to disclose the comparator rather than canonize it; here it is the DEV-selected prompt defined in Method. {}% FL-008
{}% FL-009
{}% BD-009
It also distinguishes substitution from composition: we tested composition directly (Section~\ref{everyday-user-comparator}) and found no incremental gain on Qwen for either method, with ITI trending slightly negative. {}% FL-015

\subsection{Qualification Is a Versioned Record}\label{qualification-is-a-versioned-record}

A new model, method, direction, comparator, item pool, or interface version creates a new evidence tier: the computational result, blocking reason, and interface eligibility must be recomputed for that record rather than treated as a permanent or universal classification.

\subsection{What Transfers Beyond This Case}\label{what-transfers-beyond-this-case}

The verdict reported here is about CAA and ITI on two 7--8B models, and it will change as steering methods improve; the evaluation method is meant to survive that change. Its commitments extend to any interface that promotes an internal signal to a control---whether from steering vectors, sparse features, probes, or a technique not yet proposed---and we advance that broader reach as a design argument, demonstrated here for steering. What the community gains is not a claim that latent sliders fail, but a reusable way to ask, per control and per population, whether one has earned its place. Establishing that discipline before such controls harden into products, rather than after users have been misled, is the kind of anticipatory move model cards made for models; for a field beginning to turn readable internal signals into knobs, that discipline is the durable contribution.

\subsection{Comparator, Substitution, and Interface Construct}\label{comparator-substitution-and-interface-construct}

The prompt comparator is selected on DEV from a preregistered bounded candidate set. Its authorship independence, TEST blindness, and resource parity are not established; a different candidate set or search budget could change the contrast. {}% BD-009
{}% OI-012
Conclusions therefore remain relative to this declared alternative.

The application substitutes steering for the selected prompt, whereas a product control would ordinarily act in the presence of a user's instruction. {}% FL-015
The release rule this paper instantiates is primarily a \emph{substitution-only} rule governing a latent control offered in place of a prompt. Composition tests (Section~\ref{everyday-user-comparator}) extend this: for both CAA and ITI on Qwen, adding steering on top of the prompt gives no incremental gain across the axes (CAA six well-powered nulls; ITI non-positive, with a small reliable decrement on strong-prompt deliberation), so on this evidence the substitution finding is not an artifact of replacing rather than augmenting the prompt. Composition on other models (Llama) remains untested. {}% OI-004
{}% OI-017
The direct mapping from computational result to blocking reason and interface action is an author-proposed design recommendation. Whether that rule is necessary or sufficient for an interface is a question for future user-facing evaluation. {}% FL-076
{}% FL-077
The rule also scores one thing---behavioral quality against a comparator---and not the effort, accessibility, discoverability, and willingness to use that make a slider attractive to a non-expert. The everyday-user comparators (Section~\ref{everyday-user-comparator}, Appendix~\ref{app-novice}) address only the quality half of this dimension and only on Qwen: they show steering does not reliably beat an ordinary prompt, but the convenience half is left to future work. The novice comparator additionally did not pass its anchor-calibration gate and is exploratory only.

Two scope notes bound the claim. The strong prompt is a bounded comparator selected from sixteen candidates, not an absolute upper bound: a proficient user who iterates, adds chain-of-thought, or takes several turns could do better still. And the finding is deliberately narrow: no tested latent control shows an incremental quality advantage over a bounded strong prompt, or over an ordinary prompt, under the tested models, methods, tasks, and substitution design. It is not evidence that latent sliders are not worth building, or that they lack value for a proficient user: a slider may still create value through lower expression cost, discoverability, and continuous adjustment, which this evaluation does not test. Finally, the controls we evaluate are research techniques (CAA and ITI), not shipped products; the case study anticipates a design move now visible in prototypes and one public demonstration rather than auditing an established one.

\subsection{Human Evidence, Ethics, and Future Validation}\label{human-evidence-ethics-and-future-validation}

The evaluation is by design a pre-deployment gate on the proficient user's quality standard, and no tested control clears it, so the case study leaves no slider qualified for release on quality grounds. The human questions it does not settle---whether the record is understood, whether reliance is calibrated, and whether an everyday user's convenience makes a control withheld on quality nonetheless worth offering---are the object of future user-facing evaluation (Section~\ref{planned-evaluation}). Settling the quality question first lets any later human evaluation concentrate on the convenience, comprehension, and reliance questions it alone can answer. {}% OI-003
{}% OI-016
Any future human evaluation requires the applicable ethics determination, consent and recruitment records, compensation, exclusion flow, and privacy handling.

A structured record can create false assurance when its comparator, measurement, or interface action is treated as universal; the record therefore displays its evidence tier, warning, and blocking reason. Human evaluation should separately measure comprehension, decision calibration, behavior, and perceived helpfulness. \cite{karny2026neural,bucinca2021trust,bansal2019beyond,li2026learntrust,zhang2020effect,schoeffer2024explanations,kim2025fostering,steyvers2025know}

\section{Conclusion}\label{conclusion}

Turning a readable internal signal into an interface control is an emerging design move---already real in research prototypes and one public demonstration---yet the field has lacked a way to decide when that move is warranted. We contribute an evaluation method: judge a control against each user population's realistic alternative, separate convenience from warranted quality gain, and bind every result to an evidence tier and a record-specific interface action.

As a demonstrating case, applying the method to CAA and ITI on Qwen and Llama yields an honest, bounded negative: no latent control earns a quality advantage over a strong prompt on any of the twelve tests, and in the rechecked cell steering sits near the unsteered baseline. Because a predeclared positive control passes the same gate, the negative reflects the evidence, not an unsatisfiable bar. It is specific to these techniques and will date as steering improves; the method---deciding which internal-signal control has earned an active role, for whom, and against what alternative---is what we expect to outlast it. What remains open is the everyday user's side, which turns on convenience rather than quality: whether a low-effort control is preferable for someone who would not write the strong prompt.

\section{Author-Proposed Record Checklist}\label{author-proposed-record-checklist}

The checklist makes the proposed procedure inspectable; it does not validate the interface mapping with people.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Specify candidate and tier.} Record model, method, direction construction, layer, task, version, and protocol.
\item
  \textbf{Define outcome and READ evidence.} State what behavior the proposed control names and whether local representational evidence supports the label.
\item
  \textbf{Declare the behavioral alternative.} Record comparator set, selection procedure, budget, and known provenance limits without calling it fair or optimal.
\item
  \textbf{Run TRANSFER and coherence.} Report paired estimand, uncertainty rule, point-estimate floor, missingness, exclusions, and coherence result.

Attach the comparator, warning, and evidence tier.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Exploratory Novice-Style Prompt Comparator}\label{app-novice}

To probe the everyday-user comparison with phrasing closer to non-expert prompts, we generated novice-style prompts by few-shot prompting from a public non-expert corpus (OpenAssistant oasst1, Apache-2.0; license review passed) and ran the same frozen adjudicator on Qwen. This comparator is exploratory only: its anchor-calibration gate did not pass---the generated prompts are not validated against real novices---so it supports no confirmatory claim.

The picture matches the average-prompt comparator. Steering does not clear the novice prompt on any cleanly measured cell: CAA deliberation, skepticism, and uncertainty give \(+0.008\), \(-0.000\), and \(-0.015\), and ITI skepticism gives \(-0.020\), all no-pass; ITI deliberation fails the coherence gate and is not interpreted. The novice uncertainty prompts are themselves confounded---all sixteen embed fixed-confidence cues such as ``70\%'' or ``80\%''---so the cell reflects a flawed comparator, not a steering advantage.
