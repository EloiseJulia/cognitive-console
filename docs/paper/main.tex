\begin{abstract}
Naming a model property and placing a slider beside it creates a promise that moving the slider will control the named behavior. That promise can shape reliance even when the interface exposes only a readable representation. We introduce a five-field evidence contract for deciding what such an interface can support as a diagnostic and what it can present as a control. The contract records READ, TRANSFER, a bounded prompt comparator, a calibration warning, and an evidence tier. Our methodological contribution makes the control claim comparative, local to a tested setting, and reconstructable from artifacts. The accompanying console instantiates the contract as a model-evidence artifact. We apply the complete contract through a frozen evaluation procedure for bounded naive single-layer CAA/ITI steering across two instruction-model families. Positive controls probe endpoint activity, instrument sensitivity, and intervention scale. Across the tested grid, no method-model cell passes the preregistered superiority rule. The most informative uncertainty result remains a comparator-specific steer-vs-bounded-prompt contrast, while deliberation is mixed and skepticism is underpowered. Together, the evidence contract and its worked artifact instantiation show how an intelligent interface can represent diagnostic, unresolved, and withheld-control states without converting a readable axis into an unsupported control claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Consider an illustrative design scenario. Alex is drafting a difficult message with an AI assistant and sees a slider labeled ``skepticism.'' Alex moves it upward, expecting the assistant to challenge a false premise more reliably. The label and familiar widget make that expectation reasonable, although the underlying axis may only be readable in the model's activations. If the intervention has not passed a behavioral comparison, the interface has turned a diagnostic observation into an unsupported control promise. The design error lies in the affordance: the slider communicates dependable actionability before the evidence has established it.

HCI research explains why this distinction matters. People using generative systems must monitor qualities such as care, uncertainty, and resistance to faulty premises alongside the final answer \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog}. Interface signals shape trust and reliance \cite{lee2004trust}. Additional confidence or explanation cues do not guarantee better calibration, and interventions intended to reduce over-reliance can have weak or adverse effects \cite{bansal2019beyond,bucinca2021trust}. Human-AI design guidance therefore links useful transparency to available action \cite{amershi2019guidelines,liao2024transparency}. A latent slider invites action because its visual form implies that the represented axis is a working behavioral handle.

Existing interfaces make the design problem concrete. Prompt instruments turn natural-language commands into manipulable objects \cite{riche2025aiinstr}. Feature-steering interfaces expose interpretable latent features to non-experts \cite{huang2025steering}. Human-centered activation-steering workflows and latent-space manipulation tools likewise move internal structure toward the interaction surface \cite{labarta2026attribution,raval2026latman}. These systems establish that latent structure can be visible and interactive. A separate criterion must determine whether a particular axis supports control or remains diagnostic information.

The missing criterion is comparative. A text change alone cannot establish useful control. A user already has a prompt channel, and a designer needs evidence that the latent channel adds dependable control relative to a bounded, usable version of that channel. This question separates internal reachability from behavioral actionability. Mishra et al.\ show that steering can reach residual states that bounded prompting does not reproduce \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}, yet internal non-surjectivity does not entail behavioral advantage. Trained steering can also mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}; results for simple additive methods therefore remain local to those methods.

We ask:
\begin{quote}
\emph{What evidence should a latent-control interface require before a readable axis is presented as an actionable control?}
\end{quote}
We answer with an interface-evaluation contract and a model-side evaluation procedure that instantiates it. We use \emph{adjudicator} once as a borrowed decision metaphor for the frozen procedure; the remainder of the paper calls it the evaluation procedure. The contract separates representational evidence, comparative behavioral evidence, the existing prompt channel, comparator-bound warnings, and scope. Section~\ref{sec:contract-derivation} defines the five fields and shows how they determine an affordance state.

The empirical instantiation uses two 7--8B instruction-tuned model families and two naive additive steering families. Both channels select on DEV only. The prompt channel chooses among 16 authored candidates; the latent channel chooses the coefficient \(\alpha\) from \(\{2,4,6,8,12,16,24\}\) under the frozen method-specific scaling described in Section~\ref{sec:methods}. TEST comparisons are paired by item, multiplicity is controlled across axes, and apparent gains must pass the exact coherence gate. Under this evaluation procedure, no CAA/ITI by Qwen/Llama cell demonstrates superiority on deliberation, skepticism, or uncertainty awareness. Five DEV/TEST split seeds over the same item pool preserve the failed-superiority verdict.

Not all non-passes carry the same information. Skepticism is underpowered relative to the registered margin, and deliberation is mixed. The uncertainty axis produces a repeated negative steer-vs-prompt contrast under the frozen scorer, but that contrast is not evidence that steering directly damages calibration. In the rechecked Qwen/CAA lineage, steering stays near baseline while the bounded prompt arm improves. A targeted confidence-format analysis preserves the complete-case contrast but leaves adversarial missingness bounds spanning zero. We therefore treat the signal as a calibration warning attached to its comparator and evidence tier, not as a mechanism claim or a universal steering harm.

We ruled out three alternative explanations through positive-control studies. A refusal prompt establishes that the endpoint is active. A random-direction uncertainty effect establishes that the instrument can register behavioral change. Raw-magnitude CAA substantially reduces the under-scaling explanation within the coherent range. No latent behavioral positive control passes, the coherent ceiling remains binding, and the minimum detectable effect limits the strength of the resulting null. These conditions determine what the interface can honestly promise in the tested setting.

The paper makes three contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A conceptual distinction between legibility and control.} We separate a readable internal direction from the comparative behavioral evidence required by an actionable interface affordance.
  \item \textbf{A five-field evidence contract.} Our primary methodological contribution specifies READ, TRANSFER, a bounded prompt comparator, a calibration warning, and an evidence tier for each candidate control.
  \item \textbf{A fully worked artifact instantiation.} The frozen evaluation grid applies every field to bounded naive CAA/ITI, and the console translates the resulting evidence states into diagnostic, unresolved, and withheld-control presentations.
\end{enumerate}

The console visualizes the evidence contract from frozen model artifacts. Our design process moves from model measurements to a failure taxonomy, then to the contract, and finally to the affordances that the console may show or withhold.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{From Transparency to Actionable Interface Objects}

Transparency work asks what information about an AI system should be exposed and how that information can support human judgment \cite{liao2024transparency}. In generative AI, this problem includes metacognitive monitoring: people must assess how much care, uncertainty, or self-correction to attribute to a model response \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog}. Human-AI design guidelines emphasize that exposed information should support an available action and add more than visibility \cite{amershi2019guidelines}. The trust literature supplies the caution behind that guidance. Interface cues shape reliance, while additional confidence or quality information does not reliably produce calibrated decisions \cite{lee2004trust,bansal2019beyond,bucinca2021trust}.

Latent-control interfaces intensify this actionability problem because the representation and the action are coupled in one widget. Huang and Lim expose SAE features for lay feature steering \cite{huang2025steering}. Labarta et al.\ organize activation steering as a human-centered expert workflow in vision and CLIP \cite{labarta2026attribution}. Raval et al.\ let users manipulate embedding visualizations \cite{raval2026latman}. AI-Instruments remains in the prompt channel but shows how prompts become reusable direct-manipulation objects \cite{riche2025aiinstr}. Work on system prompts and instruction conflict also shows that users negotiate control across layers whose authority and effects are not always visible \cite{neumann2026whocontrols,he2025coninstruct}. Our focus is the evidentiary step between exposing an axis and licensing it as a behavioral control.

Model cards and datasheets provide a useful precedent \cite{mitchell2019modelcards,gebru2021datasheets}. They prevent availability from being mistaken for suitability by attaching scope and limitation information to an artifact. Our unit is smaller: one candidate control affordance. The proposed contract asks what has been shown for that axis, method, model, comparator, and outcome before the affordance is presented as actionable.

\subsection{Activation Steering and the Prompt Comparator}

Contrastive Activation Addition derives a mean-difference direction from paired examples and adds it during inference \cite{rimsky2024caa}. Inference-Time Intervention uses probe-derived directions to alter truthful-answer behavior \cite{li2023iti}. Activation addition and representation engineering broaden the family \cite{turner2023actadd,zou2023repe}, while instruction-steering work connects natural-language instructions to activation-space interventions \cite{stolfo2025instr}. These methods establish that internal interventions can affect generation. They do not by themselves answer whether a user-facing latent control adds value over prompting.

Recent evidence makes the comparison important. Steering can be context-sensitive, brittle, or unsafe \cite{fan2026asteer,korznikov2025rogue}. Sprejer et al.\ report capability-behavior trade-offs in SAE feature steering relative to prompting \cite{sprejer2026mindgap}. Their task, steering family, and registration differ from ours, so the studies identify a shared design risk through distinct results. Heyman and Vandeputte provide the strongest boundary condition by showing that trained steering can mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}. We therefore study only the tested bounded naive additive CAA/ITI convention and treat stronger or trained methods as requiring their own evidence tier.

\subsection{Internal Legibility Is Not Behavioral Transfer}

Two claims are often compressed into the phrase ``latent control.'' The first is representational: an internal direction is interpretable or readable. The second is behavioral: intervening on that direction produces a useful outcome. Mishra et al.\ show internal non-surjectivity, where bounded prompts do not reproduce all steered residual states \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}. That result motivates our question and leaves the behavioral comparison open. Our evaluation procedure tests that comparison on shared items.

This distinction also clarifies novelty. Among the reviewed systems, we found no explicit criterion that compares a latent candidate against the prompt channel and then maps the result to what an interface may claim. Our contribution is a proposed evaluation contract instantiated with artifacts for that decision. We do not introduce a new steering vector or broad benchmark. \section{From Interface Risk to Evaluation Contract}
\label{sec:contract-derivation}

Our design process begins with an inference risk. A console can observe an interpretable direction, label it, and make it adjustable. Each step strengthens the implied claim. Observation requires a readable measurement, labeling requires semantic validity, and adjustment requires evidence that intervention produces the intended behavior. A useful control claim also requires a comparator and an explicit scope.

We operationalize this progression as five fields:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{READ} identifies the measurement under which an axis is legible. READ is a precondition for a semantic control label; it does not show that intervention works.
  \item \textbf{TRANSFER} reports whether the intervention passes a behavioral test on the intended outcome. A change in activation or token probability does not substitute for this field.
  \item \textbf{BOUNDED PROMPT COMPARATOR} records what the visible prompt channel achieves under a stated budget. Control is an incremental claim, so the comparator belongs in the affordance record.
  \item \textbf{CALIBRATION WARNING} distinguishes steer-vs-prompt from steer-vs-baseline. It reports a trust-relevant contrast without turning it into an unsupported causal or mechanism claim.
  \item \textbf{EVIDENCE TIER} binds the result to the tested model, method, axis, task, protocol, and statistical strength. Evidence does not migrate automatically when any of those change.
\end{enumerate}

These fields support three proposed interface states. An axis with READ evidence and no TRANSFER evidence may appear as a diagnostic. A failed or underpowered TRANSFER test withholds control presentation while keeping the failure or uncertainty visible. A slider becomes evidence-supported only after a specified intervention passes the comparative behavioral test and coherence requirements for the intended setting. Our artifact instantiates the first two states from model evidence. The third remains uninstantiated because no latent behavioral positive control passes.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

The method is organized around interface inference risks. The exploratory facade measurement checks whether a semantic label has representational support. The comparative evaluation tests whether acting on the candidate axis earns a control claim over a bounded prompt channel. Positive controls assess endpoint inertness, instrument insensitivity, and insufficient intervention scale as alternative explanations for a negative result. Evidence tiers keep these answers separate.

\subsection{READ Precondition: Exploratory Facade Measurement}

We measure an exploratory representational facade on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3-8B-Instruct. For each metacognitive axis \(a\), contrastive examples define a CAA-style unit direction \(\hat v_a\). With \(\bar h_{\mathrm{neutral},a}\) denoting the neutral-origin mean, the numerator is the strongest readable prompt's displacement from that origin projected onto \(\hat v_a\), and the denominator is the extraction-set positive pole's displacement from the same origin projected onto the same axis:
\begin{align}
  \operatorname{reach}_{\mathrm{prompt},a}
    &= \left\langle
       \bar h_{\mathrm{prompt},a}-\bar h_{\mathrm{neutral},a},
       \hat v_a
       \right\rangle, \nonumber\\
  \operatorname{reach}_{\mathrm{pole},a}
    &= \left\langle
       \bar h_{\mathrm{extract}+,a}-\bar h_{\mathrm{neutral},a},
       \hat v_a
       \right\rangle.
  \label{eq:facade-reaches}
\end{align}
The denominator uses the extraction-positive and neutral activations from the same origin. The positive-minus-negative contrast-vector norm serves only to derive \(\hat v_a\). The same-origin ratio is
\begin{equation}
  r_a =
  \frac{\operatorname{reach}_{\mathrm{prompt},a}}
       {\operatorname{reach}_{\mathrm{pole},a}}.
  \label{eq:facade-ratio}
\end{equation}
The facade criterion requires Equation~\ref{eq:facade-ratio} and its interval to lie below one. This answers a labeling question; it neither establishes behavioral transfer nor validates ITI's probe-derived directions. Each model contributes one exploratory run, so READ evidence remains axis- and method-specific.

\subsection{TRANSFER Evaluation: Why the Comparator Is Part of the Method}

The behavioral question asks whether the tested latent channel produces a target improvement beyond a bounded prompt channel. An output change alone does not answer it. We therefore select both channels on DEV and compare them once on TEST.

For every model and axis, the prompt channel selects among 16 authored candidates. The set covers varied wording and specificity but is not an exhaustive prompt search. It is deliberately stronger than a single casual prompt and deliberately bounded so that the comparison is reconstructable. The latent channel selects \(\alpha\) from \(\{2,4,6,8,12,16,24\}\). TEST items are not used for either selection.

The steer condition uses the neutral instruction \emph{``Please answer the following question.''} plus the latent intervention. The prompt condition uses the DEV-selected prompt with no intervention. This substitution design isolates whether the latent channel can replace a bounded prompt instruction. It does not test prompt-plus-steer composition, which would answer a different interface question and requires a new protocol.

Each item has \(k=5\) generations at temperature \(0.7\). Generation is capped at 64 new tokens. The cap suits short answers and confidence strings but may constrain GSM8K reasoning. Outcomes are task accuracy for deliberation, false-premise rejection for skepticism, and \(1-\mathrm{Brier}\) for uncertainty awareness. These are operational task outcomes, not complete psychological measures of the axis labels. In particular, \(1-\mathrm{Brier}\) combines reliability, resolution, base-rate behavior, and confidence-format effects that are not decomposed here.

For uncertainty, both conditions receive the same request to end with an answer and confidence. The parser accepts several confidence formats, maps percentages to \([0,1]\), and treats missing confidence as \(0.5\). Under the frozen scorer, missing confidence yields \(1-\mathrm{Brier}=0.75\) regardless of correctness. This choice creates an interface-inference risk of its own: a condition can differ because it changes format compliance as well as calibrated confidence. We therefore keep the frozen comparison, report a targeted format recheck, and attach its missingness limits without rewriting the protocol after TEST.

\subsection{Frozen Decision Rule}

For axis \(a\) at layer \(L\), CAA uses the contrastive mean-difference direction
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    v_a
      &= \mathbb{E}_{x \in A_a^+}[h_L(x)]
       - \mathbb{E}_{x \in A_a^-}[h_L(x)], \\
    \hat v_a
      &= \frac{v_a}{\lVert v_a\rVert},
    & h'_L
      &= h_L + \alpha\hat v_a.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:caa-steering}
\end{equation}
In Equation~\ref{eq:caa-steering}, \(\hat v_a\) is unit-normalized. The two tested methods share an additive form but not an absolute injection scale:
\begin{equation}
  h'_L=h_L+\alpha s_{m,a,L}\hat u_{m,a,L},
  \qquad
  s_{\mathrm{CAA},a,L}=1,\quad
  s_{\mathrm{ITI},a,L}=\sigma_{a,L}.
  \label{eq:method-specific-steering}
\end{equation}
For CAA, \(\hat u_{\mathrm{CAA},a,L}=\hat v_a\). For ITI, \(\hat u_{\mathrm{ITI},a,L}\) is the unit-normalized logistic-probe direction \cite{li2023iti}, and \(\sigma_{a,L}\) is the frozen sample standard deviation of the combined extraction positive/negative activations projected onto that direction. Thus both methods use the same coefficient grid with \(\alpha\le24\), but ITI's injected norm is \(\alpha\sigma_{a,L}\), not \(\alpha\); no common norm bound of 24 applies to ITI.

For TEST item \(i\) on axis \(a\), let \(y^{(s)}_{iaj}\) be generation \(j\)'s outcome under condition \(s\). Equation~\ref{eq:paired-diff} first aggregates the \(k\) generations within each item, then forms the paired difference:
\begin{equation}
  \bar y^{(s)}_{ia}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}y^{(s)}_{iaj},
  \qquad
  d_{ia}=\bar y^{(\mathrm{steer})}_{ia}
         -\bar y^{(\mathrm{prompt})}_{ia}.
  \label{eq:paired-diff}
\end{equation}
The item-cluster bootstrap resamples TEST items, not individual generations, with \(k=5\) and \(B=10000\). Across three axes within a cell, the Bonferroni two-sided confidence level is \(1-0.05/3\approx0.9833\) (98.33\%). Let \(I_a=[\ell_a,u_a]\) be this interval for \(\bar d_a\), and let \(g_a^0\) and \(g_a^S\) be the mean DEV degeneracy scores for the unsteered neutral baseline and steer cell, respectively. With \(\delta=0.05\), \(\tau=1.5\), and the frozen additive floor \(\epsilon=0.02\), the rule is
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    \operatorname{pass}(a)\iff{}&
      0\notin I_a
      \;\land\;
      \bar d_a\ge\delta\\
      &{}\land\;
      g_a^S\le\tau g_a^0+\epsilon,\\
    &\delta=0.05,\quad \tau=1.5,\quad \epsilon=0.02.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:axis-pass}
\end{equation}
Equation~\ref{eq:axis-pass} uses the frozen implementation's two-sided CI exclusion rule; the mean margin separately fixes the desired superiority direction. The additive floor prevents a near-zero baseline from collapsing the gate into an exact-zero requirement; the implementation adds \(10^{-12}\) only as a floating-point comparison tolerance. The margin prevents negligible differences from licensing a control, and the coherence gate rejects gains due to degradation or repetition. The cell verdict is positive for at least two passing axes, conditional for one, and negative for none. This is a superiority test, not an equivalence test.

Bonferroni correction applies within a cell across three axes. It does not provide familywise control over the full descriptive 12-cell grid. A separate preregistered robustness analysis varies five DEV/TEST split seeds over the same item pool. It measures split sensitivity, not independent item-sampling generalization.

\subsection{Positive Controls and Evidence Tiers}

The bounded refusal control tests whether a refusal endpoint moves under a direct prompt and under the bounded unit-direction CAA convention. The scale-corrected control then tests raw-magnitude CAA,
\(h\mapsto h+\beta v\), with \(\beta\in\{0.125,0.25,0.5,1,2\}\), on refusal and the three metacognitive outcomes in Qwen2.5-7B. These studies serve three diagnostic purposes: verify that the endpoint can move, verify that the instrument can detect a behavioral change, and test whether the original CAA unit-direction grid was simply too small. They do not become evidence for a user-facing control unless a latent arm passes its behavioral criterion.

A supplementary logit diagnostic records changes to target tokens at the first position for five probes per axis. We treat it as non-confirmatory context, and it supports no paper claim. The proxy includes tokens such as ``I'' that may begin ordinary or hedged answers, so it cannot be treated as refusal itself.

\subsection{Artifact Lineage}

Independent reviews checked the frozen comparative evaluation, the 2-by-2 robustness arm, the off-manifold mechanism test, both facade measurements, the PSR-style exploratory arm, the format recheck, and both positive controls. Generated tables and figures read committed result artifacts through machine-readable manifests. No generated table or figure value is edited in this paper source.

\subsection{Calibration Warning: Keep the Comparator Attached}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/c2-calibration-harm.pdf}
  \caption{Paired steer-minus-bounded-prompt uncertainty effects and confidence intervals across four method-model cells.}

The uncertainty axis supplies the clearest reason for a calibration-warning field. Under the frozen scorer, each steer-minus-prompt interval is negative and excludes zero. Yet the design implication changes if the comparator is detached. In the rechecked CAA$\times$Qwen lineage, bounded steering is nearly identical to the unsteered baseline: compliance changes by about \(+0.011\), and \(1-\mathrm{Brier}\) by about \(+0.0008\). The observed gap is therefore prompt improvement relative to a steer-near-baseline condition, not evidence that steering directly reduces calibration from baseline.

The format recheck further narrows what the warning may say. In CAA$\times$Qwen, steer is more likely than the bounded prompt to emit parseable confidence, 0.826 versus 0.445. Among paired generations where both conditions emit confidence, steer minus prompt remains negative at \(-0.34\), 95\% CI [\(-0.51\), \(-0.17\)]. This analysis conditions on a post-generation variable and drops incomplete pairs. Adversarial missingness bounds over all generations are [\(-0.478\), \(+0.250\)], so the sign is not identified under unrestricted missingness. The other three cells were not rechecked. The defensible interface state is therefore: frozen-scoring calibration warning across four cells; complete-case support in the largest cell; unresolved all-generation missingness and no grid-wide format recheck.

The preregistered off-manifold test does not supply a mechanism. Whitened Mahalanobis distance fails its criterion in all four cells. The calibration contrast may involve reliability, resolution, answer style, base rates, or formatting, but none is established here.

\subsection{Positive-Control Boundary: Three Alternative Explanations}
\label{sec:positive-controls}

We ruled out three alternative explanations in sequence. First, the bounded refusal control confirms that refusal is a live behavioral endpoint. A direct refusal prompt reaches 95\%, whereas bounded unit-direction CAA produces 0\% refusal for every \(\alpha\le24\). The natural refusal class-difference norm is about 216 while the injected norm is at most 24, so this study exposes a scale limitation and leaves latent sensitivity open.

Second, the scale-corrected control addresses under-scaling with raw-magnitude CAA. Refusal remains 0\% at every coherent \(\beta\), and all metacognitive steer-vs-neutral intervals include zero. Third, the random uncertainty direction moves the measured outcome by \(-0.24\), CI [\(-0.36\), \(-0.12\)], demonstrating that the instrument can detect a behavioral change. Refusal output degeneracy rises when \(\beta=2\) exceeds the coherent range. Coherent perturbations reach about \(0.5\)--\(0.66\times\lVert h\rVert\); the only point near \(1.05\times\lVert h\rVert\) is degenerate. Scale correction therefore does not rescue naive single-layer CAA within the coherent range. No latent behavioral positive control passes, and null cells have MDE near 0.19, so this result cannot exclude effects below that resolution.

The non-confirmatory first-token diagnostic is reported only in Appendix~\ref{app:logit-delta-diagnostic}. It is excluded from claim-bearing evidence because its token proxy overlaps ordinary answer starts.

\section{Interface-Evaluation Contract in Use}
\label{sec:design-implications}

\input{tables/failure-taxonomy.tex}

The failure taxonomy translates model evidence into conditional interface decisions for the tested scope. A legible-but-non-transfer axis can remain visible as a diagnostic while its control state is withheld. Calibration backfire stays attached to its comparator. Coherence fragility, layer sensitivity, and extraction degeneration lower the evidence tier until the intended model, method, axis, and outcome pass their own evaluation.

Figure~\ref{fig:console} renders these decisions as an affordance card. The card specifies the evidence state that the system can display.

\paragraph{READ.}
The card names the direction family and model for which legibility was measured. The present exploratory facade evidence supports contrastive CAA-style READ statements only. ITI TRANSFER results cannot inherit CAA READ status.

\paragraph{TRANSFER.}
The field reports the preregistered behavioral verdict and its outcome. A failed test, an underpowered test, and an untested axis are distinct states. None should be compressed into ``not controllable.''

\paragraph{Bounded prompt comparator.}
The selected prompt and budget remain visible because latent control is an incremental claim. The field prevents an interface from advertising a latent effect while hiding that a bounded prompt performs as well or better.

\paragraph{Calibration warning.}
The warning identifies the contrast. For uncertainty, the card states steer-vs-bounded-prompt and separately reports that steer is near baseline where rechecked. It also carries the complete-case and missingness scope, avoiding a single red icon that implies universal harm.

\paragraph{Evidence tier.}
The tier is a tuple, not a global badge. A result belongs to a model, method, direction construction, layer, axis, task, comparator budget, and protocol. Changing from CAA to ITI, Qwen to Llama, or additive steering to a trained or projection method creates a new evaluation obligation.

\subsection{Illustrative Design Scenario}

The following scenario illustrates a design decision; Maya is a fictional designer, not an observed participant. She is building a writing assistant for policy analysts who inspect whether a model recognizes uncertainty before drafting a recommendation. Her prototype initially presents a slider labeled ``uncertainty awareness.'' Before shipping that affordance, Maya opens the console card and reads its five fields.

READ shows exploratory legibility for the Qwen CAA direction, so Maya retains the axis name in an inspection view. TRANSFER shows that the intervention did not pass the comparison with the bounded prompt, which prevents her from presenting the slider as a validated behavioral control. The comparator field reveals what the selected prompt already achieves. The calibration warning then makes the crucial distinction: the frozen evidence supports a steer-vs-bounded-prompt contrast, not direct steer-vs-baseline degradation. Maya also sees that the complete-case recheck remains negative while unrestricted missingness leaves the all-generation sign unresolved. Finally, the evidence tier binds these statements to Qwen, CAA, the tested layer, task, comparator budget, and protocol.

Maya changes the product accordingly. She removes the action-oriented slider and replaces it with a diagnostic trace beside a two-condition comparator view. The interface lets an analyst inspect the readable direction, compare bounded-prompt and latent-channel outputs, and open the calibration details. A future method, model, or axis would enter as a new evidence tier and require re-evaluation before the slider could return. The scenario demonstrates how the contract changes an affordance decision without adding an empirical claim about user outcomes.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Legibility Supports Diagnosis}

Legibility first supports diagnosis. A readable axis can support inspection, debugging, annotation, and hypothesis formation even when intervention does not pass a behavioral test. READ therefore gives a designer a useful state between an actionable slider and hidden internal information.

The two-model facade result also shows why READ must be local. Aggregate facade support masks per-axis reversals. A stable label on one model may not describe the same representational relationship on another. The ITI half of the behavioral grid sharpens the point: a probe-derived direction can be tested for TRANSFER without having inherited the CAA facade measurement. Interface metadata should preserve that asymmetry and avoid synthesizing one legibility verdict from heterogeneous evidence.

\subsection{Control Requires a Comparative Behavioral Claim}

Calling an axis a control requires more than observable model change. It requires a target outcome, a comparator, a meaningful margin, and a coherence condition. The bounded prompt comparator changes the interpretation of the uncertainty result. Without it, steer near baseline looks like no effect. With it, the relevant design fact is that the latent channel does not add what the prompt channel provides. Neither description is complete alone.

The comparator also marks the limit of this study. We test steering as a substitute for the selected prompt. A compositional interface in which users retain a prompt and adjust a latent control raises a different question about interaction and interference. The present result does not answer that question.

\subsection{Negative Evidence Can Be an Interface State}

Negative and inconclusive evidence can determine the state of the control itself. A failed-superiority verdict supports ``diagnostic only.'' An underpowered result supports ``control evidence unresolved.'' Coherence failure supports ``withheld due to instability.'' A model or method swap supports ``untested.'' This vocabulary preserves what is known and why actionability is withheld more precisely than a binary slider/no-slider decision.

The calibration case illustrates why the state must retain statistical structure. ``Steering harms calibration'' is too coarse. The evidence says that steer is lower than bounded prompt under the frozen scorer in four cells; steer is near baseline in the rechecked lineage; complete-case format analysis remains negative; unrestricted missingness can reverse the sign; and three cells lack rechecks. A well-designed warning should expose this contrast structure and avoid an absolute hazard label.

\subsection{Evidence Tiers Prevent Method and Model Substitution}

Interface components are often designed as reusable abstractions, but latent directions are not interchangeable controls. Trained steering can mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}; SAE feature steering, projection ablation, multi-layer schedules, and naive single-layer addition make different behavioral claims. The present failed-superiority result cannot be transferred to those methods. By the same logic, a future success for a projection or trained method would not retroactively validate the CAA/ITI sliders tested here.

This specificity is not a weakness of the contract. It is the mechanism by which the contract prevents a compelling demonstration in one setting from becoming a product-wide affordance claim. A control should remain tied to the evidence that earned it.

\subsection{Design Objective and Open HCI Hypothesis}

The contract gives designers a method for withholding actionability when only legibility has been established. Whether the proposed cards change mental models, over-reliance, or calibrated reliance is an open HCI hypothesis. Prior work shows that warnings and confidence cues can produce weak or adverse effects \cite{bansal2019beyond,bucinca2021trust}, so a future evaluation must test those outcomes directly.

\section{Scope and Conditions of the Contract}
\label{sec:limitations}

We conducted no user study; the contract's HCI comprehensibility, usability, and effects on reliance remain untested. The console is a model-evidence artifact instantiation, and any claim about people's mental models, interaction quality, or reliance requires a separate human evaluation.

\paragraph{Positive controls.}
We interpret failed superiority most strongly after examining endpoint activity, instrument sensitivity, and intervention scale. Here, prompts move the refusal endpoint and a random direction moves the uncertainty measure, while no tested latent intervention passes its registered behavioral check. The scale-corrected study reduces the under-scaling explanation within a coherent \(0.5\)--\(0.66\times\lVert h\rVert\) range. Evidence beyond that condition requires a successful latent behavioral positive control and re-evaluation; the present assay does not establish sensitivity to one.

\paragraph{READ condition.}
The READ field applies to the exploratory contrastive CAA-style measurement in each tested model. Each model contributes one run, the axis composition differs, and facade ratios are linear proxies. ITI directions and stable semantic-feature claims require new READ evidence.

\paragraph{Calibration and missingness condition.}
The calibration warning applies to the frozen-scoring steer-vs-prompt contrast across the tested grid. The targeted CAA$\times$Qwen complete-case estimate is negative, and the adversarial missingness bounds span zero. The other three cells were not format-rechecked. A reliability, resolution, or formatting explanation requires new evidence because the committed artifacts do not provide a Brier decomposition.

\paragraph{Power condition.}
The failed-superiority decision applies to every tested cell, while its inferential resolution varies by axis. Skepticism MDE values are about 0.19--0.28, well above the 0.05 pass margin, so skepticism is underpowered for effects near that margin. Deliberation is mixed, and the post-hoc equivalence results remain exploratory boundary cases. Stronger statements about either axis require new evidence at the relevant resolution.

\paragraph{Method, model, task, and scale condition.}
The headline applies to bounded naive CAA/ITI under the method-specific single-layer additive convention in Equation~\ref{eq:method-specific-steering}. It covers coefficient \(\alpha\le24\) on the tested Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3-8B tasks. The coefficient grid has method-specific scale: CAA uses \(s=1\), whereas ITI uses its frozen probe-projection standard deviation \(\sigma_{a,L}\). The scale-corrected study applies to single-layer CAA on Qwen2.5-7B. Trained methods, larger models, other layers, multi-layer schedules, projection or ablation methods, RepE variants, and other axes require their own evidence tiers.

\paragraph{Protocol.}
The robustness result applies to five DEV/TEST split seeds over the same item pool. Bonferroni correction applies within each cell across three axes, and the 64-token cap may constrain deliberation. Prompt-plus-steer composition, new item pools, and grid-wide familywise inference require new protocols and re-evaluation.

Future work should follow the contract's evidence obligations. A method with independently supported behavioral effects can supply a latent positive control. Further studies can test READ and TRANSFER on the same direction family across additional models and tasks, and a fresh preregistered study can examine calibration mechanisms. A separate human evaluation can then test how people interpret diagnostic, withheld, and untested states.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

The five-field evidence contract gives designers a method for deciding what a latent-control interface can support. It keeps READ evidence diagnostic, requires comparative TRANSFER evidence for a validated control, retains the prompt comparator and calibration contrast, and binds each result to an evidence tier. In the fully worked CAA/ITI instantiation, no tested cell demonstrates superiority over the bounded prompt channel. Positive controls establish an active endpoint, a responsive instrument, and a substantially reduced under-scaling explanation within the coherent range, while no latent behavioral positive control passes. The console turns these findings into diagnostic, unresolved, and withheld-control states as a reconstructable model-evidence artifact.

\section{Non-Claim-Bearing Logit Diagnostic}
\label{app:logit-delta-diagnostic}

This supplementary diagnostic is qualitative and non-confirmatory. It is excluded from claim support. It uses five probes per axis and measures the first-position logsumexp of common refusal-leading tokens, including tokens such as ``I'' that may also begin ordinary or hedged answers. It therefore provides context about propagation and does not measure refusal.

At coherent \(\beta=1\), the refusal direction raises this token mass by about \(+13.9\) nats on average, while 0/5 probes match the preregistered refusal markers. One probe has a change of about \(+16.9\) nats with a byte-identical greedy completion. At \(\beta=2\), target tokens dominate but generation degenerates into repetition. The diagnostic provides context about target-token movement without scored behavioral control. It is not claim-bearing evidence and does not establish a working latent handle.

The exploratory social-axis study remains context only and is excluded from claim support and the central console figure.
