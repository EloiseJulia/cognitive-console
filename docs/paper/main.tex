\begin{abstract}
Interfaces that make large language models more legible often invite a stronger promise: if users can name or visualize a cognitive axis, perhaps they can steer behavior by moving along the same latent direction. We test that promise as a reality check rather than as a new steering method. Motivated by internal non-surjectivity as background \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}, we separate representational readability from behavioral control. First, an exploratory measurement finds a representational facade for several metacognitive axes in Qwen2.5-7B: readable prompts occupy only part of the corresponding extracted activation direction. We then use a frozen, fairness-controlled, independently audited DEV/TEST adjudicator to compare bounded best-prompt control with naive off-the-shelf CAA and ITI steering across Qwen2.5-7B and Llama-3-8B. Across this tested grid, latent steering does not beat the prompt ceiling on the adjudicated axes, and the uncertainty axis consistently harms calibration; see the generated results table and calibration-harm figure. These findings do not imply that all steering fails, especially given trained prompt-mimicking steering \cite{heyman2026steer}. They instead argue for cognitive consoles that surface the limits and failure modes of legible control, helping users recalibrate trust rather than offering a latent ``control slider.''
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
A growing class of language-model interfaces asks users to treat model behavior as something they can inspect, name, and adjust. Prompt instruments reify natural-language commands as manipulable interface objects; transparency work asks how non-experts should understand model capabilities and uncertainty; feature-steering interfaces suggest that latent directions may become usable controls \cite{riche2025aiinstr,liao2024transparency,huang2025steering}. These systems respond to a real HCI need. When people use generative AI for reasoning, search, drafting, or decision support, they are not only asking for an output. They are also trying to monitor whether the model is being careful, skeptical, calibrated, or merely fluent \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog}.

The hard question is whether a readable axis is also a safe control primitive. A latent direction can look meaningful to an analyst: it can separate contrastive prompts, align with an interpretable label, or move generated text in a recognizable way. But user-facing control requires more than recognizability. It requires that acting along the direction improves the behavior that matters, under a fair comparison to what a user could already do with prompts. For a cognitive console, the distinction is not semantic bookkeeping. If a console turns a legible direction into a slider, users may infer that reading the model's state and steering the model's behavior are two sides of the same instrument. Our thesis is that this inference can be false: a cognitive axis can be legible in representations yet fail as a behavioral control, and naive latent steering can even make calibration worse.

Prior theory gives a reason to be suspicious, but not a result we can simply import. Mishra et al. show internal non-surjectivity: activation steering can reach residual states that bounded prompting does not reproduce \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}. We use that workshop result as motivation for a prompt/latent gap at the internal-state level. It does not, by itself, prove that prompting and steering diverge on user-visible behavior. Conversely, the strongest counterpoint is not hypothetical. Prompt-steering-recovery work shows that trained steering can mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}. Our claim is therefore deliberately narrow: it concerns bounded prompt effort versus naive, off-the-shelf CAA and ITI steering on metacognitive axes for the two tested model families. It is not an impossibility theorem for latent control.

We make this narrow claim through a frozen behavioral adjudication rather than an anecdotal steering demo. The protocol selects both the best prompt and the steering strength on DEV, evaluates on disjoint TEST items, compares paired item-level outcomes, controls multiplicity across axes, and applies a coherence gate before crediting a latent win. The comparison is designed to answer the reviewer question that matters for an interface: if a non-expert already has a bounded prompt channel, does an interpretable latent direction add behavioral control on top of that channel? The answer in our tested setting is a qualified but consequential no. Across the full CAA/ITI by Qwen/Llama grid, latent steering does not exceed the bounded best-prompt ceiling on the adjudicated axes, and the calibration-harm finding on uncertainty replicates across the grid. A concurrent study by Sprejer et al. reaches a related degradation pattern with different machinery, using SAE features and MMLU rather than our pre-registered CAA/ITI behavioral adjudication \cite{sprejer2026mindgap}. We treat that work as external corroboration, not as a scoop.

This negative result is useful because it changes what a console should promise. The design implication is not that latent methods are useless, nor that users should be denied model-state information. It is that an HCI system should instrument the boundary between legibility and control. A console can show where a direction is readable, where it fails to transfer to behavior, where calibration is harmed, and where stronger or trained steering would need a separate frozen test. Such a console supports trust recalibration: it helps users learn when a legible model state is diagnostic evidence and when it is not a control affordance.

This paper contributes:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An exploratory representational measurement.} We measure a representational-facade pattern on Qwen2.5-7B metacognitive axes, showing that readable prompts can occupy only part of an extracted axis direction. This claim is explicitly exploratory and single-family pending replication.
  \item \textbf{A frozen prompt-vs-latent behavioral adjudication.} We provide a pre-registered, fairness-controlled, independently audited comparison of bounded best-prompt control against naive CAA/ITI steering across the tested Qwen/Llama method--model grid.
  \item \textbf{A generalized scoped negative with calibration harm.} Under that adjudicator, naive latent steering fails to beat the bounded prompt ceiling in every tested cell, while uncertainty calibration is harmed; quantitative values are reported only in the generated table and figure.
  \item \textbf{A design stance for cognitive consoles.} We argue that HCI tools should surface limits, failed transfer, and trust-calibration risks rather than presenting legible latent directions as user-facing control sliders. This is a design implication from model evidence, not a user-study claim.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

\subsection{Non-surjectivity and Prompt--Latent Mismatch}
Our work starts from a gap between two meanings of control. In the prompt channel, a user expresses an intention in natural language and observes the resulting behavior. In the latent channel, an analyst or system intervenes on internal representations. Mishra et al. provide the theoretical background for why these channels need not coincide: steered residual activations can be internally non-surjective with respect to bounded prompts \cite{mishra2026nonsurj}. We do not treat that workshop result as our empirical finding. Instead, we ask whether a related gap appears at the behavioral level, under a frozen comparison between the best available bounded prompt and naive latent steering.

The main counterpoint is prompt-steering recovery. Heyman and Vandeputte show that trained steering can mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}. That result sharply limits any broad reading of our negative finding. We therefore scope our contribution to off-the-shelf CAA and ITI, not to learned or optimized steering methods. The planned latent-recovery arm is the direct response: if a trained steering procedure recovers prompt behavior under the same adjudicator, it would not invalidate our frozen negative result for naive steering, but it would identify a different class of latent control.

\subsection{Activation Steering Lineage}
Activation steering has developed a rich toolkit for turning representational directions into interventions. Contrastive Activation Addition constructs mean-difference directions from paired examples and adds them during inference \cite{rimsky2024caa}. Inference-Time Intervention uses probe-derived directions to alter truthful-answer behavior \cite{li2023iti}. Activation addition and representation engineering broaden this family of techniques and motivate the idea that interpretable latent coordinates can be operationalized as controls \cite{turner2023actadd,zou2023repe}. Instruction-steering work is especially close because it links natural-language instructions to activation directions \cite{stolfo2025instr}.

Our experiment does not dispute that these interventions can change outputs. It asks a stricter interface question: when the comparator is the best bounded prompt selected under the same DEV/TEST discipline, does the latent intervention improve the target behavior without degrading usable output? Recent work on steerability limits and safety failures similarly cautions that latent interventions can be brittle, context-dependent, or harmful \cite{fan2026asteer,korznikov2025rogue}. Sprejer et al. independently report capability--behavior trade-offs when steering SAE features \cite{sprejer2026mindgap}. Their method, tasks, and registration status differ from ours, which is why we frame their result as corroborating evidence for the broader risk rather than as the same contribution.

\subsection{HCI, Transparency, and Trust Neighbors}
HCI research has long treated AI interaction as a problem of legibility, control, and calibrated reliance. Work on metacognitive demands in generative AI explains why users need to monitor not only what a model says but how confidently or carefully it appears to reason \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog}. Human-centered transparency roadmaps argue that explanations and controls must be designed around what users can act on, not merely what systems can expose \cite{liao2024transparency}. Studies of system prompts, instruction conflicts, and confidence-signal recalibration further show that users negotiate control across visible and hidden layers of AI behavior \cite{neumann2026whocontrols,he2025coninstruct,li2026learntrust}.

Several interface neighbors make parts of the same design space concrete. AI-Instruments reifies prompt-layer actions, but does not introduce a latent channel \cite{riche2025aiinstr}. Huang and Lim study layperson interfaces for SAE feature steering and persona building, a strong HCI neighbor to our console framing \cite{huang2025steering}. Labarta et al. present a human-centered activation-steering workflow for vision/CLIP expert debugging rather than LLM generation \cite{labarta2026attribution}. Latent Manipulator steers embedding visualizations rather than model behavior \cite{raval2026latman}. Work connecting latent space, steering vectors, calibration, and trust shows that the phrase ``latent control'' is already crowded, but does not supply a prompt-vs-latent behavioral adjudication for metacognitive axes \cite{subramani2026latent}. Our distinctive move is to make the prompt channel and the latent channel compete under a frozen behavioral test, then translate the resulting boundary into a console design stance.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

Our method separates two questions that are easy to conflate in a console design. C1 asks whether a readable prompt occupies the same representational direction as an extracted cognitive-axis pole. C2 asks whether acting on a legible latent direction improves behavior beyond what a bounded prompt channel already achieves. The first question is exploratory and representational; the second is a frozen behavioral adjudication. We keep them separate so that a positive legibility signal cannot be silently promoted into evidence of user-facing control.

\subsection{C1 Facade Measurement}
The C1 analysis measures a representational facade on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. For each metacognitive axis, we construct contrastive activation directions from positive and negative examples and ask how far the strongest readable prompt travels along the extracted axis. The measurement uses the generated C1 artifact at \pathtt{results/gpu\_7b\_2026-07-23/c1/}, with figure provenance recorded in \pathtt{docs/paper/figure-manifests/c1-ratio-ci.yaml}. It is not a behavioral user study and not a confirmatory multi-model result.

The plotted quantity is the scale-free facade ratio
\begin{equation}
  r_a = \frac{\mathrm{prompt\_reach}_a}{\mathrm{pole\_reach}_a},
  \label{eq:facade-ratio}
\end{equation}
where the numerator is the projection of the strongest prompt-origin activation shift onto axis \(a\), and the denominator is the reach of the extracted axis pole at the chosen non-degenerate layer. A small ratio means that the prompt is semantically legible but reaches only part of the corresponding extracted activation direction. The figure also includes the random-null reference on the same scale, so that the reader can distinguish a meaningful prompt-aligned displacement from what would be expected from unrelated directions. Because this run covers one model family and shows axis heterogeneity, we treat C1 as exploratory evidence for a measurement pattern rather than as a general law about prompts and activations.

\subsection{Frozen Behavioral Adjudicator}
The C2 adjudicator is frozen in \pathtt{docs/ledgers/prereg-c2b-adjudication.md} and reused by the robustness arm in \pathtt{docs/ledgers/prereg-robustness-mechanism-arm.md}. Its purpose is to test a control claim under a fair comparator: if a user or interface already has a bounded prompt channel, does naive latent steering add behavioral control beyond the best prompt selected under the same discipline?

The protocol fixes the outcome for each axis before seeing TEST results: deliberation is scored by task accuracy, skepticism by false-premise rejection, and uncertainty by the proper per-item score \(1-\mathrm{Brier}\) (Table~\ref{tab:axis-outcomes}). Focus is excluded from C2 because the C1 extraction diagnostics did not support treating it as a valid facade axis. For each remaining axis, items are split into DEV and TEST pools from the same task distribution. Both channels get their search on DEV only: the best prompt is selected on DEV, and the steering strength is also selected on DEV. The TEST set is used only once for the paired adjudication.

This design deliberately removes several researcher degrees of freedom. DEV-only selection prevents the latent channel from benefiting from a post-hoc best steering strength and prevents the prompt channel from being weakened by an under-tuned comparator. Paired TEST differences compare the two channels on the same items, reducing cross-sample noise and avoiding the weaker logic of unrelated confidence intervals. The item-cluster bootstrap respects the repeated samples nested within each item. Bonferroni correction across axes controls the chance of declaring a latent win by trying several metacognitive targets. The meaningful-margin threshold prevents tiny but statistically detectable differences from being written as practical control. Finally, the coherence gate rejects apparent gains that arise from degraded or degenerate outputs rather than usable behavior. Together, these controls make the adjudicator a HARKing-resistant measurement instrument: success criteria, selection budget, multiplicity handling, and failure verdicts are fixed before TEST inspection.

For CAA, the latent direction is the contrastive mean difference \cite{rimsky2024caa}
\begin{equation}
  v = \mathbb{E}_{x \in A^+}[h_L(x)] - \mathbb{E}_{x \in A^-}[h_L(x)],
  \qquad
  h'_L = h_L + \alpha \hat{v},
  \label{eq:caa-steering}
\end{equation}
where \(L\) is the intervention layer and \(\hat{v}\) is the unit direction. ITI uses the same additive intervention form but obtains the direction from a probe-derived signal rather than a CAA contrast \cite{li2023iti}. For each TEST item \(i\), the frozen comparison is the paired steer-minus-prompt outcome difference
\begin{equation}
  d_i = \mathrm{steer}_i - \mathrm{prompt}_i.
  \label{eq:paired-diff}
\end{equation}
The confidence interval is obtained by paired item-cluster bootstrap: resample items as clusters, each carrying its $k$ samples, with $B \ge 10000$. For the three adjudicated axes, the per-axis CI is the Bonferroni two-sided interval at
\begin{equation}
  1 - \frac{0.05}{3} = 0.98333.
  \label{eq:bonferroni-ci}
\end{equation}
An axis passes iff
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    0 &\notin \mathrm{CI}_{0.98333}(\bar d_a) \\
    &\wedge\quad \bar d_a \ge \delta \\
    &\wedge\quad \mathrm{coherence\ ratio}_a \le 1.5,
    \qquad \delta = 0.05.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{eq:axis-pass}
\end{equation}
The frozen verdict is STRONG\_GO for at least two passing axes, CONDITIONAL\_GO for exactly one passing axis, and KILL\_PLAN\_D for zero passing axes.

\subsection{Steering Families and Models}
The robustness arm expands the original Qwen/CAA adjudication into the full CAA/ITI by Qwen2.5-7B/Llama-3-8B grid. CAA represents a simple mean-difference steering family; ITI provides a probe-direction family with a different construction lineage. Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct and Llama-3-8B-Instruct are treated as separate model families, with Llama run through the NousResearch identical-weights mirror recorded in the decision log. The arm reuses the same frozen adjudication rule rather than creating a new success criterion for each method or model. Thus, a positive cell would have narrowed the negative claim, while all-cell failure strengthens only the scoped statement about these naive, off-the-shelf steering families.

\subsection{Audit and Artifact Lineage}
Credibility is part of the method, not an afterthought. E-0005, E-0006, and E-0007 were each reviewed by independent hostile audit sessions that treated prior conclusions as untrusted and rechecked the relevant artifacts, pairing, model/method provenance, parser integrity, and aggregation. The LaTeX source does not manually transcribe result values into prose. Main quantitative artifacts are generated through manifests under \pathtt{docs/paper/table-manifests/} and \pathtt{docs/paper/figure-manifests/}; the C2 table and calibration-harm figure trace to frozen JSON artifacts, and the C1 figure traces to the registered C1 facade experiment. This lineage is what permits the paper to report a negative result without asking the reader to trust a narrative reconstruction.

Figure~\ref{fig:c2-calibration-harm} isolates the most practically striking sub-finding. On the uncertainty axis, the steer-minus-prompt effect is negative in every method--model cell, and each interval excludes zero. Thus the latent intervention is not merely failing to add useful uncertainty control; in the tested grid, it worsens calibration relative to the DEV-selected best prompt. For a console that might expose a calibration or confidence control, this is the highest-risk failure mode in the paper.

The mechanism is deliberately left open. The pre-registered OOD activation-capture test in \pathtt{docs/ledgers/prereg-ood-capture.md} asked whether off-manifold distance, measured by whitened Mahalanobis distance of steered residuals, explained the calibration harm. E-0007 returned a valid null rather than mechanism support. We therefore report calibration harm as a robust behavioral phenomenon in E-0006, not as evidence for an off-manifold causal account. No alternative mechanism is mined from the same data.

\section{Design Implications}
\label{sec:design-implications}
\input{tables/failure-taxonomy.tex}

The design implication of C2 is not to hide latent information from users. It is to change what a cognitive console treats as the unit of interaction. A console that turns every legible direction into a slider invites a control interpretation that the evidence does not support. A safer design treats legibility, transfer, and calibration as separate states that must be instrumented and displayed. This stance follows HCI work arguing that transparency should support actionability and calibrated reliance, not merely expose more internal structure \cite{tankelevitch2024metacog,liao2024transparency,li2026learntrust,neumann2026whocontrols,subramani2026latent}.

Table~\ref{tab:failure-taxonomy} turns the model evidence into five interface-relevant failure classes. F1 is the basic legible-but-non-transfer facade: an axis can be readable as a representation but fail the behavioral adjudicator. F2 is the calibration backfire documented on the uncertainty axis, where the console should warn that naive steering may reduce rather than improve trust-relevant behavior. F3 is a guardrail class: the coherence gate did not drive the present verdict, but it represents the kind of degeneration that should block deployment of a latent control. F4 captures layer sensitivity, reminding designers that a direction found at one intervention layer is not automatically a stable interface object. F5 captures extraction degeneration, where the axis itself should be redesigned before any control claim is made.

A cognitive console should therefore instrument boundaries rather than promise latent agency. At minimum, it should show whether a direction has passed a prompt-vs-latent behavioral test, whether a prompt baseline is already better, whether calibration-facing behavior is harmed, and whether the result is exploratory, confirmatory, or untested for the current model and method. These signals can help users and developers recalibrate trust: a readable latent axis becomes diagnostic evidence about the model, not a guarantee that moving along it is an appropriate user-facing action. This is a design implication from model evidence only. We do not claim that users understand these warnings, rely on them appropriately, or benefit from the console without a future human-subjects study.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The paper is best read as a reality check on an appealing HCI inference. Legibility is valuable: it gives analysts and users a vocabulary for asking whether the model appears deliberative, skeptical, or calibrated. But the frozen adjudication shows that, for the tested naive steering families and model families, that vocabulary does not automatically become a behavioral control channel. This distinction matters for interface design because the failure is not invisible. The console can expose it: representational reach, transfer verdict, calibration harm, coherence status, and artifact lineage can all be made visible instead of being collapsed into a single control affordance.

The strongest counterpoint is trained prompt-steering recovery. Heyman and Vandeputte show that steering can be trained to mimic prompting \cite{heyman2026steer}. That result is not a nuisance citation; it defines the boundary of our claim. Our C2 result concerns naive, bounded CAA/ITI-style interventions, not optimized steering procedures trained to recover prompt behavior. The planned frozen latent-recovery arm is therefore the direct next test. If a PSR-style method passes the same adjudicator, the conclusion should be narrowed rather than defended rhetorically: naive legible directions failed, while trained recovery may identify a more faithful latent control class.

Sprejer et al. provide useful corroboration from a different route \cite{sprejer2026mindgap}. Their feature-steering results also warn that moving along interpretable internal features can degrade behavior relative to prompting. The overlap increases confidence that calibration and capability trade-offs are not merely an implementation accident in our code. The differences are equally important: their steering family, task domain, and registration structure differ from ours, so the two papers should be read as converging evidence about a risk, not as duplicate demonstrations of the same empirical claim.

The generalization scope is intentionally modest. We cover two model families, two off-the-shelf steering families, and three adjudicated metacognitive axes under a frozen protocol. We do not test larger models, trained steering, multi-layer schedules, representation-engineering variants, product-specific prompts, or user adaptation to console feedback. We also do not prove the mechanism of calibration harm. The value of the result is narrower but cleaner: within a transparent and audited comparison, a legible latent direction did not beat a bounded prompt ceiling and did harm calibration.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

C1 remains exploratory. The facade ratio is measured on Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and the Llama replication is still owed before the representational claim can be promoted beyond a single-family diagnostic. The result is also heterogeneous across axes: focus is an overshoot/non-facade case, and deliberation shows layer sensitivity. These caveats are not peripheral; they are part of the reason the paper treats C1 as a measurement probe rather than as a confirmatory claim.

C2 is stronger but still scoped. The negative result covers bounded prompt effort versus naive/off-the-shelf CAA and ITI under the frozen adjudicator. It should not be cited as an impossibility theorem for activation steering, latent control, or prompt/latent equivalence. Trained or optimized procedures, including PSR-style prompt-mimicking steering, remain outside the completed evidence and are the target of the planned latent-recovery arm \cite{heyman2026steer}. Other models, tasks, axes, layers, and intervention schedules may behave differently.

The mechanism of calibration harm is open. The off-manifold distance account was not merely untested; it was pre-registered and returned a valid null in E-0007. That null leaves the behavioral harm intact but removes mechanism credit. Future mechanism work needs a new preregistered package rather than post-hoc reinterpretation of the same capture data.

Finally, the HCI claim is deliberately a design implication, not a user-study result. The human-study fork remains owner-gated because it would require separate ethics, recruitment, and venue decisions. Until such a study is run, we cannot claim that people understand the proposed warnings, calibrate trust better, or make better decisions with the console.

\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future-work}

The first follow-up is the frozen latent-recovery arm. A PSR-style or otherwise trained steering method should be evaluated under the same DEV/TEST discipline, paired bootstrap, multiplicity correction, and coherence gate. A pass would not erase the CAA/ITI negative; it would identify the conditions under which latent control becomes recoverable.

The second follow-up is representational replication. Running the C1 facade measurement on Llama-3-8B would test whether the Qwen facade pattern is model-family-specific and would clarify which axes are stable enough to motivate behavioral tests. Any new axis should first pass extraction and layer-sensitivity diagnostics before entering a control claim.

The third follow-up is mechanism work for calibration harm. Because E-0007 rejected the pre-registered off-manifold distance account, future work should test new hypotheses in a fresh preregistered package, with the mechanism claim separated from the already-banked behavioral result. Candidate mechanisms might concern confidence-format disruption, distributional shifts in answer style, or interactions between sampling and calibration scoring, but those hypotheses require new evidence.

A final branch is human evaluation. If the owner approves the human-subjects route, a study should test whether boundary instrumentation actually improves users' calibrated reliance and control judgments. The present paper supplies the model-evidence basis and the failure taxonomy; it does not supply behavioral evidence about users.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper tested a narrow but important inference for cognitive-console design: whether a readable latent direction can be treated as a behavioral control. The evidence supports three scoped conclusions. First, C1 provides exploratory single-model evidence that several metacognitive axes have a representational facade pattern, while also showing heterogeneity and an axis failure case. Second, C2 provides a frozen, audited negative result across the tested CAA/ITI by Qwen/Llama grid: naive latent steering does not beat the bounded best-prompt ceiling, and the uncertainty axis shows calibration harm in every cell. Third, the appropriate HCI stance is to surface those boundaries and trust-calibration risks rather than package legible directions as latent control sliders. We do not claim a universal limit on steering, a proven mechanism for calibration harm, or demonstrated user benefit. The contribution is a scoped reality check and a design discipline for treating legibility and control as different properties.
